## The lecture in one minute

- After the Qing dynasty fell in 1912, China broke up under rival **warlords**.
- From **1928 to 1937** the **Nationalist Party** ruled from **Nanjing**. This is the **Nanjing Decade**, a time of **nation-building**.
- A top priority was **roads and railways** in five southeastern provinces. They were built for four reasons: **unification, accessibility, commodification, identity**.
- To show off the new roads, the Zhejiang government ran the **Southeast Scenic Tour**. It took politicians, journalists, writers and **artists** to beautiful places, then published a book, ***In Search of the Southeast* (1935)**.
- The painters on the tour painted these places in the **old Chinese landscape manner**, with no cars, roads or tourists in sight.
- At the same time, ink-and-brush painting was being renamed ***guohua*, "national painting"**.
- Using culture to build a national identity is called **cultural nationalism**. That is the lecture's key idea.

## Part 1 — Historical background (slides 2–7)

### From empire to republic
- **1911–12:** "The Chinese Revolution led by revolutionary and political leader **Sun Yat-sen** (1866–1925)."
- "The young Emperor **Puyi** (1906–1967) was abdicated the imperial throne in **1912**."
- "The **Qing dynasty overthrown**."
- "The **Republic of China** was established in Beijing."

### The warlord period (1912–1927)
- "**Fragmentation of China's government** due to constant turnover of administrations."
- A 1925 map shows China as a patchwork of separate warlord territories.
- "**Mid-1920s: Many residents of Shanghai fled to the foreign concessions**", "leaving **China's cultural world largely unsupported by government authorities**".

**In plain words:** there was no stable government to pay for or protect art and culture. Remember this: it is the problem the next section's government sets out to fix.

### The Nanjing Decade (1928–1937) ★★
- "**The Nationalist Party** (Kuomintang, KMT / Guomindang, GMD)":
  - "Established the Republican government in **Nanjing** during 1927–1928."
  - "Consolidated its power in the early 1930s by **driving the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) out** of major cities and rural areas in **Southeast China**."
- "**Northeast China** was taken by **Japan and the Soviet Union**."
- The maps make one thing clear: in 1928 the Nationalists firmly controlled only a small block in the **southeast** — Jiangsu, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangxi. Their control spread outward over the decade.

**"A decade of nation-building / nationalization"** — three areas:

| Area | Slide wording |
|---|---|
| **Higher education** | "The **National Central University** in Nanjing" |
| **Art and culture** | "University curriculum, art academy, exhibition, art and painting society, etc." |
| **Infrastructure** | "**Road and railway as a high priority**" |

## Part 2 — Building roads and railways (slides 8–12) ★★

### What was built
- "The Nationalist Party placed **infrastructure construction as a high priority**."
- "**The National Economic Council (NEC)** executed an infrastructure project in **five nearby provinces in Southeast China (Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Anhui & Jiangxi)**." (Zhejiang is bold and underlined on the slide: it is the focus.)
- "Connecting the new ones to existing ones."
- "Building a **network of railways and highways**."
- Then the slide asks: "**Why?**"

### Why — four reasons, each on its own slide ("Strategic development!")

| Reason | Slide wording | In plain words | Slide's picture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unification** | "To **unify a fragmented nation** as a symbol of the Nationalist government's **sovereignty** over SE China" | Linked-up roads show that one government is in charge | Map of "Divided China" |
| **Accessibility** | "To make accessible to **previously inaccessible scenic sites**." "Zhejiang was renowned for its **picturesque landscapes** due to its mountainous topography, yet **inadequate transportation infrastructure**" | Beautiful places that nobody could reach could now be visited | **Yandang Mountain** |
| **Commodification** | "To **commodify those sites through promoting tourism**, thereby **boosting economic development** in southeast China" | Turn scenery into a tourist product that earns money | **Medicine King Mountain** |
| **Identity** | "To foster a growing **modern national identity**" | Travelling the country makes people feel it is theirs | **The West Lake** |

**Memory aid: U-A-C-I — Unification, Accessibility, Commodification, Identity.**

## Part 3 — The Southeast Scenic Tour (slides 13–18) ★★

The definition of **nationalism** (table above) comes just before this section.

### The tour
- "**Southeast Scenic Tour** 東南交通周覽會."
- "A series of scenic tours organized by the **Department of Reconstruction, Zhejiang provincial government**."
- It "brought participators to **scenic locations in and around Zhejiang**".

**Three purposes:**
1. "To **experience recently built railways and roads** in Zhejiang and four nearby provinces"
2. "To **make the infrastructure visible**"
3. "To **make hinterland into view**" (the hinterland is the remote inland countryside)

**The routes**
- "**Three routes starting from Hangzhou**, Zhejiang."
- The slide shows the original printed route map: three coloured loops spreading out from Hangzhou along highways and railways, with stars marking scenic spots. One loop goes north towards Nanjing, one east to the coast, one south-west into Jiangxi and Fujian.

**Two phases ★**

| Phase | Who | What followed |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase I** | "**Politicians, journalists, writers, and artists**" | "**Post-tour exhibition and catalogue**" |
| **Phase II** | "**Open to the public**" | — |

**In plain words:** the government first took opinion-makers on the trip so they would write, photograph and paint it. Then ordinary tourists were invited to follow.

## Part 4 — Cultural nationalism and *guohua* (slides 19–29) ★★

### Cultural nationalism
"**The mobilization of culture as a way to construct a national identity.**"

The slide shows two book covers as examples from elsewhere — ethnic music in Latin America, reading in Ireland — then asks: "**What about art in China?**"

### The answer: *guohua*
"**Chinese painting or *Guohua* 國畫** — *guo* (national), *hua* (painting). **The nationalization of ink-and-brush painting as 'the Chinese national painting'.**"

### How the label changed ★★

| | **Traditionally** | **The modern era** |
|---|---|---|
| Sorted by | "**By subject matters**" | "**By nations → nationalization**" |
| The categories | **Landscape painting**, **bird-and-flower painting**, **figure painting** | ***Guohua* (Chinese painting)** VS ***Xihua* (Western painting)** |
| Slide's verdict | "**A form of elitist art**" | "**A form of national art**" |

On the slide, the same three Chinese paintings appear twice. First each has its own genre label. Then they are pushed together under one label, *guohua*, and set against a Western oil painting.

**The Hong Kong food slides.** Two slides show pineapple buns, egg tarts, wonton noodles, egg waffles and fish balls. The first is captioned "**Street snacks in Hong Kong**", the second "**The Food of Hong Kong**".

**In plain words:** separate snacks become "*the* food of Hong Kong" once you label them by place. In the same way, landscape, bird-and-flower and figure painting became "*the* painting of China" once people started sorting art by nation. Nothing about the paintings changed. The label did.

### Why landscape painting in particular?

The slide asks: "**Why nationalizing ink-and-brush painting? Especially landscape painting?**"

**Step 1 — "By the early 1930s".** A slide shows four very different kinds of art being made at the time: oil paintings, a modernist picture, a woodcut. None is an ink landscape.

**Step 2 — the "Revivalist assertion" ★**
> "**Chinese landscape painting** was the **very foundation of China's greatest artistic tradition** due to its long tradition. It would remain so as the **central genre** of Chinese painting."

**Step 3 — Zheng Wuchang, *Chinese Painting Monthly* (December 1934) ★**
> "**China is now lagging behind other countries in all ways, military, political, and economic. The only thing about which we can be proud is our culture.** Painting holds a very important position in our traditional culture… Modern Chinese painters… should also carry out the mission of **cultural construction** and **cultural preservation**."

**In plain words:** China was weak in armies, politics and money. Culture was the one thing it could be proud of, and landscape painting was the heart of that culture. So painters were given a patriotic job.

## Part 5 — *In Search of the Southeast* (1935) (slides 30–43) ★★

### The book
- "***In Search of the Southeast*** 東南攬勝 (**1935**). An **anthology**."
- "**State-funded**, published after the Southeast Scenic Tour."
- Published by "the **Southeastern Infrastructure Tour Propaganda Group** of the **National Economic Council (NEC)**". Note the word *propaganda* in the publisher's name.

### What is in it
- "Containing **different forms of cultural productions**":
  - "**travel literature, poem, landscape photography**"
  - "**Chinese painting**, portraying the scenic location **in a traditional way**"
- The slide shows a spread from the book: photographs of a mountain road and a temple, with poems and a travel essay printed beneath.

**Table of contents**
- "Several chapters, featuring **different routes for travel along major trans-provincial highways and railways**; touring around natural scenic sites, lakes and rivers."
- "**Each route accompanied by relevant travel literature, poem, painting and photographs.**"
- It also has "**location maps, route maps, topographic maps** of scenic locations".

**In plain words:** it is half art book, half travel guide. The chapters follow the new roads.

### How the paintings show the places ★★

The slide's sentence — learn it:
> "**Many Chinese paintings depict the scenic locations in a traditional Chinese way — mountain (*shan*) and water (*shui*) elements — without any trace of modern touristic activity.**"

**In plain words:** the tour existed to advertise new roads and railways. Yet the paintings show only timeless mountains and waterfalls: no road, no train, no tourist.

### He Tianjian, *The Fourth Cataract of the Five Cataracts in Zhuji* (1934) ★
- Ink and colours on paper.
- **What you see:** a huge brown cliff climbing to a peak, mist, dark trees, a white waterfall at the bottom right pouring into rapids. **No people, roads or buildings.**

The slides then pair it with older paintings to show where its manner comes from:

| Quality | Borrowed from | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Monumentality** | "**Northern Song (10th C) landscape painting**" | One towering mountain mass that fills the picture |
| **Calligraphic brushwork** | "**Yuan and Ming (14th C to 17th C) landscapes**" | Brush lines that are expressive in themselves, like handwriting |

**Texture strokes.** "**Textural stroke: a portrayal and expressive technique in traditional Chinese painting.**" The slides show a brush diagram, a page from an old painting manual, and samples of named stroke types. A second He Tianjian painting, *Gazing at a Waterfall from Cliffs* (1948), is shown with four magnified circles picking out different strokes in one picture.

**In plain words:** He Tianjian's "modern" tour painting is built entirely from the tools of the old masters — Song grandeur, Yuan and Ming brush line, traditional texture strokes.

### Wang Jiqian (C. C. Wang), *Xiansheng Gate* (1934) ★
- Ink and colours on paper.
- **What you see:** two great cliffs almost touching, leaving a narrow gap like a gateway.
- The slide puts it beside "**A view of Xiansheng Gate (1934)**", a **photograph** of the same spot from the book.

**In plain words:** same place, same year — one seen by a camera, one turned into a traditional landscape.

### What it all meant ★★

Slide: "**The role of traditional cultural aesthetics?**"

**"The Nationalist political ideology":**
- "to **revitalize** Chinese traditional culture"
- "to **modernize** Chinese travel culture"

"**Participated by both Chinese painters and the Nationalist government.**"

**In plain words:** the project looked backward and forward at once. Traditional-style painting kept the old culture alive; roads, photographs and guidebooks made travel modern. Painters and the state worked on it together.

## Part 6 — The summary slide: "*Guohua* and Cultural Nationalism" ★★

***Guohua*:**
- "**the representative art form of modern China** ('Chineseness', geographical mobility, modernity)"
- "**Chinese national and cultural identity**"
- "**a tool for cultural propaganda, both locally and internationally**"

---

## The argument in five steps

1. China is **divided** → the Nationalists need to **unify** it.
2. They build **roads and railways** in the southeast.
3. They organise a **tour** so that the new infrastructure and the hinterland are **seen**.
4. Painters depict the scenery in **traditional landscape** style, and the state **publishes** it.
5. Ink painting becomes ***guohua***, the national art, and a **tool of cultural propaganda**. This is **cultural nationalism**.

---

## Easy to mix up

| Do not confuse | With |
|---|---|
| **Warlord period**: 1912–1927 | **Nanjing Decade**: 1928–1937 |
| **Nationalism**: loyalty to the nation-state above other interests | **Cultural nationalism**: using culture to construct a national identity |
| ***Guohua***: Chinese ink-and-brush painting | ***Xihua***: Western painting |
| Traditional sorting: **by subject** (landscape, bird-and-flower, figure) — "elitist art" | Modern sorting: **by nation** — "national art" |
| The **tour** (Southeast Scenic Tour), organised by the **Zhejiang Department of Reconstruction** | The **book** (*In Search of the Southeast*, 1935), published by the **NEC's propaganda group** |
| **Phase I**: politicians, journalists, writers, artists | **Phase II**: the public |
| **Monumentality** ← **Northern Song** | **Calligraphic brushwork** ← **Yuan and Ming** |
| **He Tianjian**: *The Fourth Cataract* | **Wang Jiqian (C. C. Wang)**: *Xiansheng Gate* |
| The Nationalists **revive** traditional culture (this week) | The Cultural Revolution **smashes** it as one of the "Four Olds" (Week 10) |
