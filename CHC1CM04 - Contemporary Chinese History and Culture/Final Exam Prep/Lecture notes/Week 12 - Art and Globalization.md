## The lecture in one minute

- **Globalization** = "free flow of capitals, human agents, and ideas around the world". It took off after the **Cold War** ended (Berlin Wall falls **1989**; Soviet Union collapses **1991**).
- In China, the 1980s were a decade of radical new art, ending with the **China/Avant-Garde** exhibition (February **1989**) and the **Tiananmen Square Incident** (April–June 1989), after which many people left.
- The lecture then looks at **two kinds of art in the 1990s**:

| | **1. Political Pop** | **2. Global contemporary art space** |
|---|---|---|
| Slide subtitle | "**To ridicule Mao's revolutionary legacy**" | "**Chinese artists outside China**" |
| Formula | "**political icons + pop art**" | Western contemporary techniques + Chinese content |
| Artists | **Zhang Hongtu** (Mao's face); **Wang Guangyi** (propaganda figures + brand logos) | **Xu Bing** (invented writing); **Cai Guoqiang** (gunpowder) |
| Key works | *Chairman Mao* (1989); *The Last Banquet* (1989); *Great Criticism: Coca Cola* (1994) | *Book from the Sky* (c. 1987–1991); *Square Word Calligraphy* (1993–1996); *Project for Extraterrestrials* |
| Key question | What happens to Mao's image after Mao? | "**How to intervene in the global order led by Western society?**" |

## Part 1 — Globalization and the end of the Cold War (slides 2–7) ★★

### Globalization
- "**Free flow of capitals, human agents, and ideas around the world.**"
- "**Collaborations between institutions and governments of different countries.**"
- "→ **A borderless world**"
- "→ **The world as a global village**"
- "→ **To transcend the geopolitical boundaries imposed by the Cold War**"
- Picture labels: "A global village"; "The Internet".
- She said: globalization is "very much **a 90s and 2000s phenomenon**".

### The Cold War world (1945–1991)
"**Geopolitical confrontation and isolation between capitalist countries and communist countries.**" "**Divide by ideology.**"

| Group | Map colour | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Capitalist countries (The Western Bloc)** | Blue | She said: led by the United States; Western Europe, Australia and others |
| **Communist countries (The Eastern Bloc)** | Red | She said: the Soviet Union, the People's Republic of China, North Korea, North Vietnam, Cuba and others |
| **Countries under the Non-Aligned Movement** | Green | She said: newly independent nations in Asia, Africa and South America, many of them former colonies, that did not want to side with East or West |

### How it ended ★★

| When | Event | Slide wording |
|---|---|---|
| — | **Berlin Wall** divides West Berlin and East Berlin | She said: people on one side could not visit or contact people on the other |
| **November 1989** | "**Collapse of the Berlin Wall** (Berlin, Germany)" | She said: an early sign of the end of the Cold War |
| **1991** | "**The End of the Cold War in 1991**": "The **collapse of the Soviet Union/USSR** in 1991" | "→ **Capitalism led the world; capitalist globalization**" |

**In plain words:** while the world was split in two, goods, people and ideas could not cross. When the Soviet side collapsed, the walls came down, capitalism spread everywhere, and the world became connected. That connected world is "globalization".

## Part 2 — China in the 1980s and 1989 (slides 8–10) ★★

### "China in the 1980s: Modern and contemporary art movements across China"
- The slide shows photos of **performance art**: artists performing on a huge sheet of painted paper, wrapped figures on the Great Wall, people burning piles of works.
- She said: after the open-door policy of **1978**, Western ideas, books and films poured in. Artists took up Western ways of making art, such as performance. The 1980s were "quite a liberating historical period".

### China/Avant-Garde, Beijing (**February 1989**) ★★
- "**National Art Museum of China, Beijing (prestigious)**"
- "**Radical artistic concepts and forms**"
- "**e.g. installation, performance**"
- The exhibition's logo, seen on the banners and posters: a **"No U-turn" traffic sign**.
- She said: this was **the museum where the Stars Group were not allowed to exhibit** (Week 11). By the end of the decade, experimental artists got inside.

**In plain words:** in 1979 the Stars had to hang their work on the fence outside. Ten years later the avant-garde was showing inside the same national museum. The "No U-turn" sign means: no going back.

### The Tiananmen Square Incident, Beijing (**April to June 1989**) ★★
- "**A massive students protest** in Tiananmen Square, **triggered by the death of a reform-minded party leader Hu Yaobang**."
- "**Political stand-off between the government and protestors → a massive bloodshed → migration and departure.**"
- She said: people with connections abroad chose to leave for Europe, America, Australia and other parts of Asia.

**Why this matters for the lecture:** "migration and departure" is why Part 4 is about "Chinese artists **outside** China".

Then the section heading: "**In the 1990s**".

## Part 3 — Political Pop (slides 12–31) ★★

"**1. Political Pop — To ridicule Mao's revolutionary legacy**"

### Step 1: where "Pop" comes from
- "**Pop — came from pop art in the USA.**"
- **Andy Warhol** (1928–1987, USA). "**Pop Art, 1960s–70s.**" "**Silkscreen print.**"
- Examples on the slide: Campbell's soup cans; a grid of Marilyn Monroe heads in clashing colours.
- She said: Warhol was struck by the rise of **mass culture**. He took icons from products and celebrity culture and turned them into art, often by **repetition**.
- **Andy Warhol, *Mao series***: "an iconic collection of paintings and screenprints created between **1972 and 1973**". Mao's official portrait repeated in unnatural colours.
  - She said: Mao was an international icon in the 1960s–70s, and 1972 was when the US president began diplomatic relations with China, which caught Warhol's attention.

### Step 2: the formula
"**Political Pop — political icons + pop art**"

| Artist | Work on the slide | The political icon used |
|---|---|---|
| **Zhang Hongtu** 張宏圖 (b. 1943) | *Chairman Mao*, 1989 | **Mao's portrait** |
| **Wang Guangyi** 王廣義 (b. 1955) | *Great Criticism: Coca Cola*, 1994 | **Worker, peasant, soldier** from propaganda posters |

- She said: Political Pop happened "not only within China, but also outside of China in the 90s".

### Step 3: the icons being made fun of (recap of Week 10)

| Slide heading | Examples |
|---|---|
| "**Cult of Chairman Mao during the Cultural Revolution**" | Liu Cunhua & others, *Chairman Mao Goes to Anyuan* (1968); Tang Xiaohe's swim painting (1972) |
| "**Cult of Chairman Mao in the Third World**" | Two posters of April 1968 showing Africans looking up to Mao: "***Savior*** 救星" and "***Chairman Mao is the great liberator of the revolutionary people of the world***" |
| "**Red Guard as A Political Icon during the Cultural Revolution**" | A photo of a rally and two Red Guard posters |

- She said: Mao was "venerated, worshipped… as an idol", in China and across the Third World. Then: "think about what happened after the end of the Cultural Revolution. How did people think about Chairman Mao?"

### Zhang Hongtu 張宏圖 (b. 1943, China-born, US-based) ★★

**His life (from the slide)**
- "Born in **Gansu Province in 1943 to a Muslim family**."
- "Graduated from the **Central Academy of Arts and Crafts in Beijing in 1969**."
- "His **father was labelled a Rightist, a counterrevolutionary in 1957**; his family was marked with the stigma of a traitor."
- "**Initially fantasized by Maoist teaching. But quickly became cynical of it**; witnessed book burnings and violence during the Cultural Revolution; **not a revolution but a destruction of culture**."
- "**Relocated to New York in 1982.**"
- "Mid-1980s to the '90s: created paintings, sculptures and mixed media installations **utilizing Mao's image** to express his ideas about Communist China and the Cultural Revolution."

**In plain words:** he believed in Mao as a boy, then watched the Cultural Revolution destroy books and people, his own family among the victims. Once he was in New York he was free to answer back, and he did it with Mao's own face.

**Work 1: *Chairman Mao* (1989)** — "acrylic on paper, collage, laid on wood board"
- **What you see:** twelve small panels, each a doctored copy of the official Mao portrait. Mao with **pigtails** like a little girl; Mao with a big **moustache**; Mao with a **tiger-striped face** and ears; Mao cross-eyed with an insect on his nose; Mao's face in pieces; Mao in a protest headband.
- **What it does (slide, in blue) ★★:**
  - "**To ridicule Mao's political sanctity by turning him into a pop icon**"
  - "**Revolutionary → Unrevolutionary portraits**"
  - "**Demystification of Mao's political power and legacy**"
- She said: this "was definitely not allowed during Mao's period China".

**The tiger panel and the "paper tiger" ★★**
- "**'Paper Tiger' as an expression**":
  - "**Something that looks terrifying at first, but is in fact powerless.**"
  - "**Used by Mao since 1946 to describe the USA and American imperialism.**"
- Two examples of the original use: a paper tiger with a US flag (*China Pictorial*, August 1950); the poster "***Expose the paper tiger***" (1958), in which a blade rips open a hollow tiger.
- **In plain words:** Mao called America a paper tiger. Zhang paints the tiger stripes on **Mao**. The insult is turned back on the man who invented it: Mao is the one who looks terrifying but is hollow.

**Work 2: *The Last Banquet* (1989)** — "laser prints pages from the Red Book and acrylic on canvas" ★★
- Modelled on **Leonardo da Vinci, *The Last Supper*** (15th century). "Zhang first [saw] this painting at the age of 14, high school."
- **What you see:** the same long table and the same layout, but **every one of the thirteen figures is Mao**. The central Mao has microphones in front of him. The cloth is red. There are rice bowls and chopsticks. One figure holds a Little Red Book. The walls are papered with pages of the Red Book. The Great Wall is in the "windows".

| *The Last Supper* | *The Last Banquet* |
|---|---|
| "**Jesus said, 'One of you will betray me.'**" | "**Mao asked, 'Who will betray me?'**" |
| Jesus and his disciples | Mao and twelve more Maos |

- Zhang's own words on the slide: "**Mao always criticized other people and he never criticized himself.**"
- She said: Mao asked everyone to criticise others, but never criticised himself. With every seat filled by Mao, the picture says **Mao betrayed himself**.
- She said: this is globalization at work. Living in the United States, Zhang freely mixes a European Renaissance painting, American pop art and Chinese political imagery.

**Vivienne Tam 譚燕玉** ★
- "**New York-based Hong Kong designer**."
- "***Mao* (fashion collection), spring/summer 1995**" — T-shirts and a dress ("a lightweight, stretchy synthetic mesh") printed with Zhang Hongtu's Mao images.
- She said: Tam collaborated with Zhang. In Mao's time his image was everywhere "for people to look up to". In the post-Mao era "**Mao became a commercial icon**" printed on fashion, which is another way to **demystify** his power and legacy.

### Wang Guangyi 王廣義 (b. 1955) ★★

"***Great Castigation* series**" (the captions call the individual works "*Great Criticism*")

| Work | Date | Political image | Brand |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Great Criticism: Coca Cola* | **1994** | **Worker, peasant and soldier** gripping one giant pen together | Coca-Cola |
| *Great Criticism: Chanel* | **2001** | **Red Guards** saluting, holding red books, against red sun-rays | Chanel No. 5 |
| *Great Criticism: Rolex* | **2005** | Soldiers with red stars, and an older man, looking up | Rolex (sold for HKD 1,000,000 at Christie's, 2010s) |
| (second "Rolex" caption, "2000s") | — | A charging crowd of soldiers | The painting itself reads **Ferrari**, and she called it Ferrari in class (sold for HKD 1,840,000) |

**The look:** flat yellow and red, heavy outlines, in the style of Cultural Revolution posters (compare the *Fully criticize the Chinese Khrushchev* poster in Week 10), with small stencilled numbers scattered across the surface and a Western logo on top.

**The slide's reading ★★:**
- "**China's economic opening since late 1978**"
- "→ **International commercial logos**"
- "→ **Employs/appropriate**"
- "→ **Critical reflections on the relationship between politics and commerce**"

**What she said about each pairing** (the image is chosen to fit the brand):
- **Coca-Cola + worker, peasant, soldier.** The three proletarian figures aim their pen at the logo, as they once aimed at political enemies. Is he criticising Coca-Cola as a commercial power spreading over the world?
- **Chanel + Red Guards.** The girls at the front salute. In the Cultural Revolution they saluted Mao. Next to the logo "it seems that they're saluting to this… luxurious brand". The old slogan was "serve the people". In the 1990s people were serving money and brands.
- **Ferrari + charging army.** The diagonal, rushing figures give a sense of **speed and efficiency**, like a sports car.
- **Rolex + soldiers looking afar.** Are these men now captured by a luxury brand?

- She said: "During the Mao's time, **politics was most important**. But… after China opened its door to the outside world, the importance of politics was **replaced by commerce and economic interests**", wealth and status.

**In plain words:** the heroes of the revolution used to attack capitalism. Wang puts them next to capitalist logos, in the same heroic poses. Propaganda and advertising start to look alike. Both tell you what to worship.

**Zhang Hongtu vs Wang Guangyi**

| | Zhang Hongtu | Wang Guangyi |
|---|---|---|
| Born | 1943 | 1955 |
| Based | New York (from 1982) | China |
| Icon used | **Mao's face** | **Workers, peasants, soldiers, Red Guards** |
| Mixed with | Pop treatment; a Renaissance painting | **Commercial logos** |
| Point | Ridicule and demystify **Mao** | Reflect on **politics and commerce** |

## Part 4 — The global contemporary art space (slides 32–59) ★★

"**2. Global contemporary art space — Chinese artists outside China**"

### Challenges outside home ★★

| Level | Slide wording |
|---|---|
| **Basic: Life** | "To adapt to a new economic model: **capitalism**"; "To overcome the **language and cultural barriers**" |
| **Artistic creation** | "To **catch up with Western contemporary art trends**"; "To **seek recognition from Western society**"; "To **position oneself in the globe**" |
| **Advanced: Philosophical level** | "**To intervene in the Western-led global order?**" |

- She said: these artists were born in the 1940s–50s and left in their thirties. Under socialism the state assigned your job and looked after you; abroad they had to find work, open a bank account, learn everything from scratch. They had been trained to paint in a socialist realist way, where art served politics.
- She said: in the international art world "the United States and Europe were the leaders", so these artists felt **marginalised**. "To intervene" means "**to change the rules of the game**".

The lecture's big question: "**How to intervene in the global order led by Western society?**"

### Xu Bing 徐冰 (b. 1955) ★★

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1981** | "Graduated from the **Printmaking Department** of the **Central Academy of Fine Arts**" |
| **1987** | "Obtained a master's degree there" |
| **1990** | "**Moved to New York**" |
| **2008** | "**Returned to Beijing**" |

"Printmaking" is underlined on the slide. It matters: both of his works are **carved in wood and printed**.

**Work 1: *Book from the Sky* 天書 (c. 1987–1991)** ★★
- **What you see:** a room-sized **installation**. Long printed scrolls hang from the ceiling in curves. The walls are covered in printed sheets. The floor is covered with rows of open, traditionally bound books.
- **The twist:** every character looks Chinese, but **none of them is real**. He invented them all and carved them by hand on wooden blocks. Nobody can read a word.
- The slide puts the installation beside photos of **big-character posters** covering walls in the Cultural Revolution.
- She said:
  - He spent about three years behind closed doors making it.
  - He grew up surrounded by big-character posters. Text was powerful, but policies and people's fortunes changed fast, so he "**developed a distrust in the function of text**" and became interested in "**demystifying the power of text**".
  - Standing inside it feels like being surrounded by big-character posters, except that you cannot read anything. "It's like a book from the sky."

**In plain words:** it looks like the most serious, official kind of writing, and it says nothing. If you grew up being told what to think by walls of text, a wall of meaningless text is a strong reply.

**Work 2: *Square Word Calligraphy* (1993–1996)** ★★ (made after he moved to the United States)

- **The idea (slide):** "**Xu reformed his mother tongue, Chinese language, by reinventing it within the mechanics and logics of English language.**"
- **How it works:** each letter of the English alphabet is redrawn as a Chinese-style brushstroke shape (his "**alphabetic table**"). The letters of an English word are then stacked inside **one square block**, so the word looks like a Chinese character.
- **Examples she decoded in class:**
  - Two big blocks + small signature = "**Square Word**" / "**Xu Bing**"
  - Four blocks = "**Art for the People**" / "**Xu Bing**"
- She said: it looks Chinese, but a Chinese reader who knows no English **cannot** read it, and an English reader who knows no Chinese **can**. He is "combining the strength of two languages". Even his signature and seal are written this way.

**It is a whole teaching system, modelled on how Chinese calligraphy is taught:**

| Part | Slide wording | What it copies |
|---|---|---|
| **Textbook** | "**Xu's self-created, carved and printed textbook**. With pages pointing out the basic writing techniques. **Bringing the audience to the origin of Chinese calligraphy**" | Shown beside "**The Eight Principles of Yong**" 永字八法: "**the eight most common strokes in standard script**" |
| **Illustrations** | "Illustration of **brushstroke order**"; "Illustration of **the manner of holding a brush**" | Traditional calligraphy manuals |
| **Copy book** | Red outlines to fill in with a brush | Traditional tracing copybooks |
| **Classroom** | "Xu Bing **invited by museums around the world** to teach his self-invented square word calligraphy"; "**Classes taught by the artist himself**" | A calligraphy classroom with desks, brushes, ink and a blackboard |
| **Audience** | "**Visitors from all around the world participating**" | — |

- She said about the Eight Principles of Yong: the single character 永 contains all the basic strokes of Chinese writing. Xu used these strokes to build his English alphabet.
- She said: because he trained in printmaking, he **carved the textbook pages on woodblocks** himself so they could be printed in quantity.

**The slide's two questions, and her answer ★★**
- "**What is *Square Word Calligraphy*'s position in the global art space?**"
- "**How did this work intervene in the global artistic order led by Western societies?**"

She said:
- Xu arrived as "a marginalized immigrant" with little English. This work "captured the **linguistic challenge**" people like him faced.
- It gives **English speakers** the experience of learning to write "Chinese". Western visitors sit or kneel at low desks and learn from him. Their bodies follow an East Asian way of writing.
- Museums around the world invited him. "He turned himself from previously a marginalized immigrant into **the center of museum attention**… **The power dynamics is reversed**."
- It is a "**participatory artwork**" that combines "the strength of traditional Chinese calligraphy" with the practice of contemporary art. That let him "strategically position himself as a global contemporary artist".

**In plain words:** normally the immigrant has to learn the West's language. Here the West has to sit down and learn *his*. That is what "intervening in the Western-led order" looks like.

**In Hong Kong:** "***Loping and Looking — Art in MTR*** (Hong Kong, 2025)". Square-word works spelling "**Admiralty**" and "**Wan Chai**" were shown in MTR stations. She said he was invited by the Hong Kong Museum of Art and the works stayed for only a few months.

***Book from the Sky* vs *Square Word Calligraphy***

| | *Book from the Sky* | *Square Word Calligraphy* |
|---|---|---|
| When | c. 1987–1991 (in China) | 1993–1996 (in the USA) |
| Looks like | Chinese | Chinese |
| Can anyone read it? | **No one** | **Yes: English readers** |
| Form | Installation to stand inside | Classroom to take part in |
| Idea | Distrust of text; demystifying the power of text | Crossing the language barrier; reversing who teaches whom |

### Cai Guoqiang 蔡國強 (b. 1957) ★

She ran through these slides quickly ("we are not going to get into this so closely"), so learn the outline.

- "**Well-known 'explosion artist' who uses gunpowder and pyrotechnics to create artworks.**"
- "**1957: Born in Quanzhou, Fujian.**"
- "**1985: Graduated from the Stage Art Department of Shanghai Theater Academy.**"

| Work | Year | Where | Slide details |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***Fetus Movement II: Project for Extraterrestrials No. 9*** | **1992** | **Germany** | "Commissioned by The **Kassel International Art Exhibition**". "Realized at a **military base** in Germany, June 27, 1992… **9 seconds**". Gunpowder (90 kg), fuse (1,300 m). Photos: digging the land; the artist sitting at the centre |
| ***The Earth Has Its Black Hole Too: Project for Extraterrestrials No. 16*** | **1994** | **Hiroshima, Japan** | "Realized at **Hiroshima Central Park near the A-Bomb Dome**… **30 seconds**". A glowing spiral of fire |
| ***Black Ceremony*** | **2011** | **Doha, Qatar** | Black smoke puffs and fireballs in the daytime sky |
| ***Flowers in the Sky*** | **2018** | **Florence, Italy** | Coloured smoke like flowers above the city |

- She said: he was invited by art exhibitions and museums all around the world, and the works are like a spectacular show. The A-Bomb Dome commemorates the atomic bombing of Hiroshima in 1945.

**In plain words:** gunpowder is a Chinese invention. Cai turns it into contemporary art events that last seconds, staged in Germany, Japan, Qatar and Italy. The list of places is the point: a Chinese artist working everywhere in the world.

## Conclusion (slides 60–61) ★★

1. "**Globalization: a borderless world; facilitated a free flow of capitals, human agents, and ideas around the world.**"
2. "**Chinese artists' adoption of Western contemporary artistic techniques, mingling them with Chinese social, cultural, and historical contents and experience.**"
3. "**Post-Cold War: Western art institutions and art markets developed interests in contemporary Chinese artists.**"
4. "**Question of self-positioning.**"
5. "**Artwork's forms and contents: manifestation of the global era's interconnectedness and artists' self-positioning strategies.**"

She said: during the Cold War China was closed to the West. Afterwards, Western museums and media became curious about what Chinese artists were doing and invited them. So each artist had to think about "how to position themselves strategically".

**How each artist fits point 2 (my summary):**

| Artist | Western technique | Chinese content |
|---|---|---|
| Zhang Hongtu | Pop art; a Renaissance composition | Mao's portrait; the Little Red Book |
| Wang Guangyi | Pop art; brand logos | Cultural Revolution poster figures |
| Xu Bing | Installation; participatory art; the English alphabet | Chinese characters, calligraphy, woodblock printing |
| Cai Guoqiang | Large-scale event art | Gunpowder |
