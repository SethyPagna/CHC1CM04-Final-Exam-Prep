## The lecture in one minute

- After losing the **Opium War**, China was forced to open **five ports** to foreign trade (1842). One of them, **Shanghai**, grew into **China's first modern metropolis**.
- Modern Shanghai had trams, cafés, cinemas, dance halls and a racecourse. It also had new ways of making pictures: **photography and lithography (printing)**.
- A new class of **rich merchants** started buying art through **dealers and shops**. Art became something you could buy off a price list.
- Painters moved to Shanghai from nearby provinces to earn a living. They are called the **Shanghai School**. They painted **new subjects** in **bright, direct, eye-catching** ways. Three to know: **Xugu, Ren Xiong, Ren Yi**.
- People also began to **picture themselves** in new ways: in **self-portraits**, and in images of **modern women** on **calendar posters (yuefenpai)** that advertised products.

**Conclusion slide, in four lines:** Modernizing Shanghai → New patronage, interests and tastes → New representation of oneself → e.g. Shanghai School painting and yuefenpai.

## Part 1 — How Shanghai became modern (slides 3–9)

### Before: a closed country
- **Late Qing dynasty (1644–1912):** "social and political upheavals, both internally and externally".
- This can be traced to the **18th century**: "**Emperor Qianlong restricted maritime and foreign trade**, limiting European commerce exclusively to the port of **Canton (Guangzhou)**."
- There were also "technological and diplomatic restrictions".

**In plain words:** for a long time foreigners could trade with China through one port only, Guangzhou.

### The turning point ★★

China "**lost in the Sino-British Opium War (1839–1842)**" → signed "**the Treaty of Nanking (1842)**".

The treaty did two things:
1. "**Ceding Hong Kong Island** to the United Kingdom."
2. "**Opening five ports for trade: Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai.**"

This began the "**treaty port era (1842–1946)**".

On the slide's map, five stars mark the ports along the coast. Guangzhou's star is a different shade from the other four, because Guangzhou was *already* the one trading port; the other four were new.

### Why Shanghai? ★

"**Shanghai: China's first modern metropolis.**"

| Reason | Slide wording |
|---|---|
| **Geography** | "Easy access to the **Grand Canal**, the **Huangpu River**, and the **Pacific Ocean**" |
| **Trade** | "Domestic and international trades" |
| **Money and people** | "Influx of capital and entrepreneurial spirit" |

**In plain words:** Shanghai sits where China's inland waterways meet the sea, so goods could flow both into China and out to the world.

### The Bund ★
- "By **1876**, more than **two hundred foreign companies** had trade connection with Shanghai."
- "Many **foreign banks** established along **the Bund**" = "**the waterfront of the Huangpu River**".
- "**Buildings in European Renaissance style**" — in blue on the slide: "**columns, domes, arches**".
- Example: **HSBC** (Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation). A photo shows the HSBC Building "shortly after opening in **1923**": a huge stone building with tall columns and a dome.
- Shanghai was "a **mercantile hub with extraterritorial administration and concessions**".

## Part 3 — Patronage: who pays for art (slides 19–21) ★★

The slide's title again says "**modern as a break from the past**".

| | **Traditional** (Ming and Qing dynasty China) | **New** (modernizing Shanghai) |
|---|---|---|
| **Who buys** | People with a **personal connection** to the artist | "A new class of **art collectors (merchant)**" |
| **How** | "**Artist residency**: inviting an artist to stay with a family for years" | Through "**art dealers and art shops**" |
| **Why they buy** | — | "To **showcase their taste and erudition**; as **social distinction**"; "art and erudite cultivation as **commodity**" |
| **What the shops do** | — | Sell "fan, ink stones, paper, mounting service"; act "**as agent**: introducing and promoting artists to buyers" |
| **How price is set** | — | "**Price list**: dependable on the **genre, size, materials, artist's fame**" |

**Two results (the arrows on the slide):**
- "→ **professional artists gaining agency**"
- "→ a new form of **urban consumption**"

**In plain words:** before, a painter depended on one family who housed him. Now a painter could sell to anyone with money, through a shop, at a listed price. That made painters independent professionals and made art a product to shop for. Merchants bought it to show they had taste.

**The next slide lists what follows from this:**
- "New mode of patronage and artistic concerns": **tastes**; **subject matters and styles**; "**turning high art into commodity**".
- "New vision of metropolis citizens": **self-portrait**; **portraits for others**; **modern ladies, e.g. calendar posters**.

Those three bullets are the plan for the rest of the lecture.

## Part 4 — The Shanghai School (slides 22–51)

### Definition ★★
"**The Shanghai School** — *Haipai* 海派 (air/attitude/style/school). **A group of artists who were active in Shanghai; not born there but relocated to there to make a living.**"

Three painters: **Xugu (1823–96), Ren Xiong (1823–57), Ren Yi (1840–95)**.

---

### Xugu (1823–96) ★

**Who he is**
- "**Religious name**: *Xu* (empty); *gu* (valley)."
- "**Inspired by the Zen style, focusing on negative space**" (another slide: "Zen Buddhist style, focusing on **emptiness and negative space**").
- "**Ordinary subject matter.**"

**New subject 1: domestic animals — *Cat and Butterflies***
- **What you see:** a white cat with orange eyes, tail up, looking at butterflies; chrysanthemums above; **large areas of paper left blank**.
- The slide's four points:
  - "**The white cat: erotic**"
  - "**A playful cat: draw people's attention**"
  - "combined **watercolor effect + a calligraphic technique bone line**"
  - "Inspired by the Zen style, focusing on negative space"
- A second cat painting and a **goldfish** painting make the same point. Goldfish: "**→ auspicious**" (lucky). "**Recurrent emphasis on negative space**": three or four small fish in a big empty space.

**New subject 2: delicacies — *Album of Various Subjects* (1895)**
- Three leaves, labelled **Litchi**, **Water chestnut / caltrop**, **Peach**. The slide adds photos of real water caltrops so you can recognise the painted shapes.
- The slide's two points:
  - "**Colorful: eye-catching**"
  - "**Formalist: many geometric repetitions, almost an abstract pattern**"

**In plain words:** Xugu painted everyday, likeable things — a cat, goldfish, fruit — that a merchant buyer would enjoy. His trademark is **empty space**.

---

### Ren Xiong (1823–57) ★★

**Who he is**
- Born in Xiaoshan, **Zhejiang**.
- "Studied both **elite literary arts** (poetry, painting, music) and **practical skills** (archery, wrestling and horse riding)."
- "Associated to men of higher social and economic status. **Some of them were his patrons.**"
- "Although **a very short life (about 34 years)**, he was considered as **one of the most innovative Chinese artists of the nineteenth century**, due to the formation of **the new style of figure painting of the Shanghai school**."

**New subject: Chinese folklore — *Magu Presenting Longevity***
- **What you see:** a tall woman in a **vivid red robe** holding a tray with a cup; an attendant holds a big lotus leaf.
- "***Magu***: a legendary **Daoist immortal** associated with the elixir of life; **the symbol of longevity** in Chinese art."
- "A red mineral pigment called '**cinnabar**': **eye-catching**."
- "**Not much cultural allusion, immediacy: you see what you see.**"

**In plain words:** you do not need to be a scholar to enjoy it. A bright red figure who means "long life" is understood at a glance.

***The Ten Myriads***
- Album leaves of landscapes on a **gold ground** with strong blue, green and white.
- "**Gold leaf: traditionally for decorative purpose, rarely used in painting.**"
- "**Luxuriousness in materials (mineral pigments).**"

**The making of new portraiture**
- Heading: "**photography and lithograph as new modes of visual technology**".
- ★ "**Portraiture in Imperial China:** Unlike European art, **self-portrait is a genre uncommon in China**. Traditionally, it was **produced for the emperor or the deceased for commemorative purpose**."
- Examples: two portraits of the **Qianlong Emperor** by **Giuseppe Castiglione** (18th century) — one formal on the throne, one as a scholar in his study.
- The slide then asks: "**How to characterize yourself in a portraiture?**"

#### *Self-Portrait* (c. 1856) ★★

**What you see:** a full-length standing man facing you. Shaved head, thin face, **staring straight out**. His **chest and one shoulder are bare**. A big blue-grey robe, drawn in **thick, angular black lines**, slips off his shoulder. Hands clasped at the waist. Wide trousers, big shoes. Empty background. A long inscription down one side.

A comparison slide sets it beside Ren Xiong's own painting "after" the older master **Chen Hongshou (1598–1652)** and a work by Chen himself: robed scholars drawn in long flowing lines. The self-portrait breaks from that older manner.

**The slide's analysis, point by point:**

| What the slide says | In plain words |
|---|---|
| "**Monumentalizing the figure**": "five feet tall", "head is small, feet are big" | The figure is nearly life-size and built like a monument |
| "**Highly naturalistic: shading and light reflection → echoes with the arrival of photography** as a new technology in China" | The body is shaded to look real, like the new photographs |
| "Abnormally thin or weak"; "**look deadly serious, staring out at the viewer**" | He looks ill and looks you in the eye |
| "**Partially covered body (traditional figures are fully covered)**" | Showing bare skin broke with tradition |
| "**Folded hands: latent energy**" | Still, but tense |
| "Fine hands with long fingernails: a sign of '**man of literati**' instead of a workman or a farmer" | He marks himself as an educated man |
| "Holding a sword: a visual pun, martial artist, which is traditionally unusual" | He also presents himself as a fighter |
| "→ Effect: **strong, tough, realistic for future remembrance**" | — |
| "→ **powerful statement of self; projection of subjectivity**" | The picture says "this is who I am" |

**Significance (its own slide):**
- "**Diagnosed with TB**, projecting a sense of **uncertainty**."
- "This self-portrait is meant to be **his autobiography**, showing **his view of the world**."
- "**Justification of traditional brushwork for documentary purpose**" — he uses the traditional brush to record a real person truthfully.

### Ren Yi / Ren Bonian (1840–95) ★

**Who he is**
- "Born into the family of a **rice merchant**, who also liked to paint."
- "Learned from his father the art of **portrait painting**. However, his father died during the **Taiping Rebellion** in about 1861. Thereafter, Ren Yi chose to pursue a career as a painter."
- "Also from Shaoxing, **Zhejiang**."
- "**The student of Ren Xiong's younger brother**, who is also a painter."

**Four portraits — he paints other people, often with a twist.**

**1. *Portraits of the Artist's Grandparent-in-law* (1885)**
- An elderly couple seated side by side; a white crane in front.
- "**Crane: immortal, auspicious to celebrate birthday.**" "**Color: bright.**"

**2. *Portrait of Gao Yongzhi* (1887)**
- **Gao Yongzhi (1850–1921):** "Ren Yi's **contemporary**"; "successful in the creation of painting in **traditional orthodox school**. He is **well-established in art market**."
- A man in a white robe sits beneath a huge pine tree. The inscription says he is about 37.
- "**Pine trees**: evergreen, longevity; symbolize **virtue of gentlemen**; lofty scholar."
- A zoomed-in detail is labelled "**Realistic face**".

**3. *Portrait of Gao Yongzhi as a Calligraphy Beggar* (1887)** ★★
- The **same man**, now shown walking in profile with a bamboo staff, straw sandals and a basket.
- "**Holding basket: traditional painting tools such as paper, brush, and ink stone.**"
- "**Selling painting as if a courtesan selling smile.**"
- "**→ social ambivalence: an artist selling art.**"
- A slide with no words places this painting beside a photograph of real street pedlars.

**In plain words:** a successful market painter is shown as a street pedlar whose goods are his brush and paper. It is a joke with a sting: is selling your art for money respectable, or is it like begging? That mixed feeling is the "social ambivalence".

**4. *Portraits of Wu Changshi: The Shabby Official* (1888)** ★★
- **Wu Changshi:** "from a **gentry family**"; "a member of the **Metal and Stone School** in Shanghai, highly competent in **calligraphy, carving, poetry, and painting**"; "during the Taiping Rebellion, his parents died. Then he had to **make a living by taking commissioned work**."
- **What you see:** a man standing stiffly, facing you, in an official's hat with a red fringe, hands tucked in his sleeves. The slide puts a photograph of the real Wu beside it.
- "**A bitter and pitiful face → to reflect his psychological anxiety of serving at the Qing court.**"
- "**Watery color: the 'boneless style'**" — the jacket is a wet wash with no outline.
- "**→ Satirize the sitter.**"

**Do not mix up the two satirical portraits:**

| | *Calligraphy Beggar* | *The Shabby Official* |
|---|---|---|
| Sitter | **Gao Yongzhi** | **Wu Changshi** |
| Pose | Profile, walking | Frontal, standing |
| Technique | Quick ink line | Watery "boneless" colour |
| Point | **Ambivalence about selling art** | **Anxiety about serving the Qing court** |

## Part 5 — Modern women and yuefenpai (slides 52–62)

### Yuefenpai ★★ (her sample question)

The slide, in full:
- "***Yuefenpai* (calendar poster/print)**"
- "**1910s–1930s**"
- "**Calendar printed in a poster format, featuring pretty ladies, sometimes celebrities, in addition to the commodities.**"
- "**Inserted in Sunday newspapers; wide circulation.**"

**Her sample question and answer**

> Which of the following is a correct feature of yuefenpai (calendar posters)?
> A. Painted on gold leaf — B. **Featuring commodities** — C. Sold through art dealers — D. Popular from the 1850s to the 1890s
>
> **Answer: B.** She explained that these posters "were meant to promote the products", and that the modern ladies "helped viewers… keep the poster for a longer time, because they were pretty ladies to be looked at".

Notice where the wrong answers come from: **gold leaf** is Ren Xiong; **art dealers** is Shanghai School patronage; the dates are wrong (it is **1910s–1930s**).

**What a yuefenpai contains** — the slides take one poster apart:
1. a **brand name** across the top;
2. the **products** themselves, pictured in the borders;
3. **slogans** down the sides;
4. a **calendar** along the bottom;
5. in the middle, the main picture: **a fashionable young woman**.

**The posters shown** (no need to memorise brands; notice the pattern):

| Product advertised | The woman |
|---|---|
| Perfume and powder | Traditional jacket and skirt, round fan, in a garden doorway |
| Tea | Short hair, sitting on a balustrade picking flowers |
| Wines, spirits and cigars (a Hong Kong firm) | Permed hair, tight qipao with a high slit, high heels, champagne beside her |
| Electrical goods | Cropped hair, fur-trimmed dress, beside an electric heater and a gramophone |
| Cigarettes | Two women dancing together in a ballroom, short dresses, high heels |
| Cigarettes | Qipao and curls, beside a jockey at the racecourse |

**In plain words:** across the posters the women's hair gets shorter, their clothes tighter and more Western, and their surroundings fill with modern things — electric heaters, gramophones, ballrooms, racecourses. The posters sell products by showing what a **modern woman** looks like.

### Magazines, advertisements, cartoons
- ***Liangyou / Young Companion*** no. 34 (**January 1929**): "Cover featuring **Anna May Wong**, American Chinese Hollywood film star."
- A six-panel **cartoon** captioned "**On Materialism**": a man on his knees offers a woman flowers, then other gifts, and she ignores him each time. When a fat older man appears holding a bag of money, she turns and embraces him.
