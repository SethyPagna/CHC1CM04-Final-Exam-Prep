## The lecture in one minute

- The question: **how can a work of art show a sense of modernity?**
- In the early 1900s many young Chinese went abroad to study. For artists, **Paris was the centre of the art world**.
- Paris offered **two kinds of art**:
  - the **Art Academy**: realistic, careful, built on anatomy and light-and-shadow;
  - **Modern Art**: free colour and line, not trying to copy reality.
- Two Chinese painters studied at the **same Paris academy** and came home with **opposite answers**:

| | **Xu Beihong** (1895–1953) | **Lin Fengmian** (1900–1991) |
|---|---|---|
| Chose | The **Art Academy** way: **realism** | The **Modern Art** way: **expression** |
| Keywords | Anatomy, chiaroscuro, three-dimensionality; plaster casts, live models, still life | Liberation of forms, colour and lines; Post-Impressionism, Fauvism, German Expressionism |
| Final slide | "**Art for social and political sake**" | "**Art for art's sake**" |

If you learn one thing from this lecture, learn that table. Her sample question tests exactly it.

## Words you need

| Word | Slide wording | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| **Modernization** | "**The process of transforming**, often characterizes **a break from the past**, from a traditional, rural, and agrarian society to a secular, urban, and industrial one" | The **process** of becoming modern |
| **Modernity** | "**The quality or state** of being or appearing to be modern, often characterizes **a break from the past** as a result of **industrialization, urbanization, and the use of technology**" | The **condition** of being modern |
| **Art Academy** | "Popular across Western Europe since 18th C" | Official art schools teaching strict realistic drawing and painting |
| **Naturalistic** | "To imitate nature" | Looking like the real thing |
| **Physiognomy** | — | The features of the face |
| **Anatomy** | — | The structure of the body: bones and muscles |
| ***Chiaroscuro*** 明暗對比法 | — | Strong contrast of light and dark, used to make things look solid |
| **Three-dimensionality** | — | Looking solid and rounded rather than flat |
| **Foreshortening** | — | Drawing something that points towards you so the near part is big and the length looks squashed |
| **Plaster cast** | — | A white plaster copy of a statue, used for drawing practice |
| **History painting** | — | A large painting of a scene from history, with many figures |
| **Fable** | — | A traditional story with a moral |
| **Post-Impressionism, Fauvism, German Expressionism** | — | Three modern European movements that use strong colour and visible brushwork to express feeling |
| **Nude** | — | A picture of an unclothed human body |
| **Lyricism** | — | A poetic, gentle mood |

**Modernization vs modernity — the difference in one line:** modernization is the **process**; modernity is the **quality or state**. Both involve "a break from the past".

## Part 2 — Background (slides 8–9)

**"Political, social and economical instability":**
- **First Sino-Japanese War (1894)** → **Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895)**, "ceding Taiwan, Penghu, and Liaodong Peninsula to the Empire of Japan".
- **The Chinese Revolution of 1911**, "overthrowing the Qing dynasty and created the Republic of China in Beijing".
- **A warlord period (1912–1927):** fragmented government, constant turnover; "mid-1920s: many residents of Shanghai fled to the foreign concessions".

**Studying abroad ★**

"Intellectuals, writers, artists studying abroad (early 20th century)." Paid for by "**personal funds**" or "**government sponsorships**".

| Went to | Who (year) |
|---|---|
| **Japan** | Chen Duxiu (1901), **Lu Xun** (1902), Gao Jianfu (1906), Yu Dafu (1913), Guo Moruo (1914), Zhou Enlai (1917) |

## Part 3 — Paris and its two kinds of art (slides 10–15) ★★

"**Paris was the center of the art world. Two sectors.**"

| | **Art Academy** | **Modern Art** |
|---|---|---|
| When | "Popular across Western Europe **since 18th C**" | "Emerged in France in **late 19th C**" |
| Subjects | "**History, real people and life, fables**" | — |
| How it looks | "**Highly realistic and naturalistic** (to imitate nature)"; "**Physiognomy and anatomy** of the human body"; "***Chiaroscuro*, three-dimensionality**" | "**Liberation of visual language instead of imitating the reality**" |
| The school | The National School of Fine Arts (**École nationale supérieure des beaux-arts**) | — |
| How students learned | A photo from the late 1800s shows a crowded studio of men at easels drawing a **nude model** | — |
| Slide's example | **Jacques-Louis David**, *Napoleon at the Saint-Bernard Pass* (1801): Napoleon on a rearing horse, smooth finish, solid figures | **Matisse**, *Woman with a Hat* (1905): a face in green, yellow and pink patches; *Dance* (1910): flat red figures on blue and green |

**The five things to compare** (heading of the comparison slides): **line, color, light & shading, space, three-dimensionality.**

| | Art Academy | Modern Art |
|---|---|---|
| Line | Hidden inside smooth modelling | Bold, visible outlines |
| Colour | True to life | Free: a face can be green |
| Light and shading | Careful, makes volume | Little or none |
| Space | Deep, convincing | Flattened |
| Three-dimensionality | Strong | Weak, on purpose |

A second comparison slide makes the same point with two still lifes: one highly finished and realistic, one in heavy black outlines and flat colour.

## Part 4 — Xu Beihong (1895–1953) (slides 17–42) ★★

### His path

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| — | "Native of **Yixing, Jiangsu** province" |
| **1916** | "Entered the French Department of **Fudan University**, met reformist **Kang Youwei** and studied under him" |
| **1917** | "Studied Fine Arts in **Japan**" |
| **1919** | "With a **government scholarship**, studied at **l'École nationale supérieure des beaux-arts in Paris, France**" |
| **1926** | "Returned to China; **one of the very few Chinese artists to have earned a formal degree from that art academy**" |

- **Teacher in Paris:** **P. A. J. Dagnan-Bouveret** (1852–1929), "an influential French **academic** artist". The slide puts Xu's oil of a woman playing the violin beside his teacher's *In the Forest* (1892): same warm tones, same academic naturalism.
- **Career:** the South China Arts Academy; **the National Central University** (newly established in Nanjing).

### Early work under Kang Youwei
- ***Kang Youwei and His Family*** (1917), ink and colour on paper. The faces are shaded like photographs.
- ***He Zhanli / Lily Haw*** (1910s), gouache and watercolour — **Kang's third wife**. The slide sets a detail of the painted face beside a **photograph** of her: they match closely.
  - Slide notes: "**Focus on realistic sketching**"; "**Attention to the spirit of truth-seeking**".
  - The portrait is then placed beside a **Ming dynasty** ink painting of a lady: Xu's is shaded and solid, the Ming one is flat and linear. (This is Kang's argument from Week 3, shown in pictures.)

### What he learned in Paris

| Slide | What it shows | Terms on the slide |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-portraits** | A photo of Xu beside a painted and a drawn self-portrait | "***Chiaroscuro*, Physiognomy, Three-dimensionality**" |
| **Sketches** | A drawing of a classical statue; *Drawing of a Female Nude* (1924, charcoal and white chalk) | "***Chiaroscuro*, Anatomy, Three-dimensionality**" |

### *Sound of the Flute* (1926) ★
- Oil. A young woman plays a long bamboo flute against a hazy golden background.
- Compared with **Ni Zan, *Six Gentlemen*** (Yuan dynasty), an ink landscape with a wide empty middle.
- Slide: "**Emptiness. A combination of the aesthetics of traditional Chinese painting and Western oil painting.**"

**In plain words:** even in a Western oil painting, Xu leaves a big open space the way Chinese ink painters do.

### How he taught ★★
"**Approaches:**"
- "implemented **European academic curriculum: plaster casts, live models, still-life painting**"
- "**copying old masterpieces**"

### His art theory (1927) ★★
> "**Artists, like scientists, are guided by the search for truth.** Just as mathematics is **the basis of science**, **figure drawing provides the foundation for art**."

> "**Artist's responsibility is to represent the beauty of nature.**"

**In plain words:** art is like science; its job is truth. And you get to truth by learning to draw the human figure accurately.

### His three themes — each "× nationalism" ★★

**1. History painting × nationalism — *Tian Heng and His 500 Retainers* (1928–30)**
- Oil on canvas, **198 × 355 cm** (very large).
- **The story** (from the slide):
  - **Tian Heng** was a leader at the end of the **Qin dynasty** (3rd century BCE).
  - He was **defeated by Liu Bang**, founder of the Han dynasty.
  - He **fled to a remote island with 500 followers**.
  - He **bade farewell and killed himself rather than surrender**.
  - **All 500 men took their own lives out of absolute loyalty.**
- **What you see:** a man in a dark red robe raises his clasped fists in a farewell salute to a large crowd of followers.
- **Western models shown:** David's *Coronation of Napoleon* (1806–07) and **Delacroix's *Liberty Leading the People*** (1830) — huge multi-figure paintings of history with a national message.
- **In plain words:** Xu uses a European form (the big history painting) to tell a Chinese story about **loyalty and refusing to surrender**.

***Awaiting the Deliverer* (1933)**, oil — a group of thin, ragged peasants all looking up and away, waiting. The slides zoom in on the bodies, with the label "**Plaster casts, live models**" and his two quotations. The point: the accurate muscles and ribs come from academic training.

**2. Fable × nationalism — *The Foolish Old Man Removes the Mountains* (1940)**
- **Ink and colour on paper** — Chinese materials, not oil.
- **What you see:** a row of large, nearly naked, muscular men swinging heavy rakes to dig; an old man talking to a woman at the left.
- Slide notes: "**copying old masterpieces**"; "**A champion of 'new *guohua*'**".
- A slide labelled "**live models**" puts his nude life drawings beside the painted diggers: the poses match.
- **In plain words:** a Chinese ink painting of a Chinese fable, but the bodies are drawn with Western anatomy from live models. That mix is the "**new *guohua***".

**3. Horse × nationalism**
- "**Focus on realistic sketching. Horses from different viewing angles: side, back, foreshortening.**" "**Truth-seeking.**"
- "Sketches in 1928", then a slide of diagrams explaining **foreshortening**.
- ***Horse in Ink*** (1939) and ***Horse in Ink*** (1944): ink paintings of a standing horse and a galloping horse, with accurate anatomy and foreshortened legs.

**The pattern in all three:** Western realist skill + a Chinese subject + a patriotic meaning.

## Part 5 — Lin Fengmian (1900–1991) (slides 43–50) ★★

### His path

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| — | "Grew up in a **rural village near Guangzhou**" |
| **1918** | "**Also** studied at **l'École nationale supérieure des beaux-arts in Paris**" — the same school as Xu |
| — | "**Strong interest in the expressive power of Post-Impressionism, Fauvism, and German Expressionism**" |
| **1925** | "**Invited by Cai Yuanpei** to return to China for serving as the **principal of Peking Art College**" |
| **1928** | "Participated in the founding of **Hangzhou National Academy of Art** (now China Academy of Art) and served as the **director and a professor**" |

A slide shows one example of each movement, all with strong, non-natural colour and visible brushwork. Another puts a still life by the **French Fauvist Maurice Vlaminck** beside **Lin Fengmian's *Still Life and Apple*** (1910s): both use thick black outlines, flat colour and a tilted table.

### Her sample question ★★

> What were the aspirations and characteristics of Lin Fengmian's artistic practice?
> A. Anatomy, chiaroscuro, and three-dimensionality
> B. Emptiness, ink, and calligraphic brushwork
> C. **Liberation of forms, color, and lines**
> D. Plaster casts, live models, and still life
>
> **Answer: C.**

What she said when going through it:
- Both men "were trained around the same time in France… But despite their similar training… they came back with **very different… understanding of modernizing Chinese art**."
- "**Xu Beihong thought that realism was very important**", with "a scientific way to analyse the anatomy… and three-dimensionality".
- "**Lin Fengmian thought that liberating… forms, color, and line from the prescribed function of describing things realistically was important.**"
- She called this "slightly more challenging" because it tests an **artistic concept**.

Where the wrong answers come from: **A** and **D** are Xu Beihong (his look, and his teaching method). **B** is the vocabulary of traditional Chinese painting ("emptiness" appears on the slide about Xu's *Sound of the Flute*).

### His three strands ★★

| # | Strand | Work | Slide wording | Compared with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **German Expressionism** | ***Suffering / Pain / Agony*** (1929), oil on canvas (**destroyed**) | "**To comment on social reality. To express a modern consciousness.**" | **Emil Nolde**, *The Life of Christ: Crucifixion* (1911) |
| 2 | **Fauvism — Nude** | ***Nude*** (1934) | "**A symbol of the West, of the modern, and of freedom**" | **Henri Matisse**, *Large Reclining Nude* (1935) |
| 3 | **Lyricism** | ***Riverside*** (1938) | "**Combining Chinese and Western aesthetics**" | Emil Nolde, *The Evening* (1916) |

**More on each:**

1. ***Suffering*** — only a black-and-white photo survives. Pale, twisted naked bodies emerge from darkness. A second slide puts it beside a photograph of prisoners being led through a street, captioned "**Persecution and driving the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) out of major cities and rural areas in Southeast China**". So the "social reality" it comments on is that persecution.
   - **In plain words:** he bends and distorts bodies to show pain, not to describe how people look.
2. ***Nude*** — a seated female nude in thick dark outlines and ochre-brown paint. The nude itself is the message: it stands for **the West, the modern, and freedom**.
3. ***Riverside*** — hills in sweeping black and brown strokes above bands of pale water. It looks like ink handled with a Chinese brush, used for a moody Western-style landscape.

**Careful:** "art for art's sake" does **not** mean Lin never touched society. *Suffering* comments on social reality. The label describes his overall aim: freeing form, colour and line.

## Easy to mix up

| Do not confuse | With |
|---|---|
| **Modernization** = the *process* | **Modernity** = the *quality or state* |
| **Art Academy**: since the 18th century; realistic | **Modern Art**: late 19th century; liberation of visual language |
| **Xu Beihong**: anatomy, chiaroscuro, three-dimensionality; plaster casts, live models, still life; truth-seeking | **Lin Fengmian**: liberation of forms, colour, lines; Post-Impressionism, Fauvism, German Expressionism |
| Xu's mentor in China: **Kang Youwei**. Teacher in Paris: **Dagnan-Bouveret** | Lin was invited home by **Cai Yuanpei** |
| Xu taught at the **National Central University, Nanjing** | Lin headed **Peking Art College**, then co-founded the **Hangzhou National Academy of Art** (1928) |
| *Tian Heng* = **history painting** × nationalism (oil) | *Foolish Old Man* = **fable** × nationalism (ink) |
| "**Emptiness**" → Xu's *Sound of the Flute* (Chinese + Western) | "**Lyricism**" → Lin's *Riverside* (Chinese + Western) |
| ***Suffering*** → German Expressionism → social reality | ***Nude*** → Fauvism → the West, the modern, freedom |
| **Emil Nolde** = German Expressionism | **Henri Matisse** = Fauvism |
| Lin's *Nude* (1934): a symbol of freedom | Yuan Yunsheng's nudes (1979, Week 11): where freedom was stopped |
