## The lecture in one minute

- **Avant-garde** = the "front guard": artists who stand at the front and push for change.
- In 1929 the **First National Exhibition** was held in Shanghai. Many artists were unhappy with the "official" style it promoted. The result: **art societies of all kinds** sprang up.
- The lecture follows **two** avant-garde groups of the 1930s:

| | **Storm Society** 決瀾社 (1931–1935) | **Modern Woodcut Movement** (1930s) |
|---|---|---|
| Medium | **Oil painting** | **Woodcut print** |
| Where | **Shanghai** | Started in Shanghai; spread to **Hangzhou, Beijing, Guangzhou** |
| Who | Painters trained in **Japan and France**: **Ni Yide**, **Pang Xunqin** | Left-wing young artists, led by the writer **Lu Xun**: **Jiang Feng**, **Zhang Wang**, **Li Hua** |
| Manifesto? | **Yes** (October 1932) | **No** |
| Interest | "The spirit of the modern age; **westernized**" | "Modern life as **a collective struggle for a better future**" |
| Approach | "Creating a **pure formal** world"; "Liberating lines, colors and shapes" | "Representing the **underrepresented groups**" |
| Slogan idea | **Art for art's sake** | Art as **social activism** |

Both resist "a stale representationalism". They do it for different reasons and in different ways.

## Part 1 — What "avant-garde" means (slide 2) ★★

- "French but common in English."
- "***Avant*: front; *garde*: guard**."
- **Four characteristics:**
  1. **Iconoclastic** — "strongly opposing generally accepted beliefs and traditions"
  2. **Individualistic**
  3. **Self-expressive**
  4. **Idealistic**

The slide's pictures: a painting of a cavalry charge with an officer at the very front (the original military meaning of "front guard"), then a striking striped stage costume and a flowing white modern building (modern things that look ahead of their time).

**In plain words:** the word began as a military term for the soldiers who go first. In art it means the people who go first and break the rules.

A divider slide then gives the map of the lecture: "**Medium: oil painting, woodcut print | design. Cities: Shanghai, Hangzhou, Beijing, Guangzhou.**" First half: **oil painting in Shanghai**. Second half: **woodcut print in all four cities**.

## Part 2 — The First National Exhibition and its discontent (slide 4) ★

- "**Took place in Shanghai, 1929.**"
- Artists were "**dissatisfied with the official construction of certain artistic practices as the recognized style** in the national exhibition".
- "→ **proliferation of art societies of all kinds**."

The pictures show the exhibition's own publication (two volumes titled "The National Fine Arts Exhibition of 1929") lying open at traditional ink landscape paintings.

**In plain words:** the government held a big national show and, by choosing what went in, told everyone which kinds of art counted. Artists who disagreed formed their own groups. The Storm Society and the woodcut movement are two results.

## Part 3 — The Storm Society 決瀾社 (1931–1935) (slides 5–21) ★★

### The basics

| | |
|---|---|
| Name | **Storm Society** (*Juelan she*) 決瀾社; *juelan* = "**breaking wave**" |
| Years | **1931–1935** |
| Members | "**Japan and France-trained painters who returned to China**" |
| | Japan: **Ni Yide** 倪貽德 (1901–70) |
| | France: **Pang Xunqin** 龐薰琹 (1906–85) |
| Base | **Shanghai** |
| Aim | "**Hit the rotten art of contemporary China with a powerful wave**" |
| Advocacy | "**Art for art's sake**"; "**Reformists, introducing a radical notion of modernism**" |

### The Manifesto (October 1932) ★★

> "Our entire art world today is **decadent and diseased**. We can no longer tolerate this compromising climate. We cannot simply allow [art] to die.
> Let us arise!
> With our raging passion and iron intellect, **we will create a world interwoven with color, line, and form**!
> The twentieth-century Chinese art world must **bring forth a new atmosphere**."

The two phrases in bold are bold on the slide. Learn them.

**In plain words:** Chinese art is sick. We will cure it by building art out of **colour, line and form** themselves, not out of copying what things look like.

### Exhibitions
- "**1932–1935: organized four exhibitions.**"
- "Showcasing works inspired by **contemporary trends in Europe**."
- "**Impact diminished since 1933.**"
- The slide shows a magazine spread: "A special feature on The Storm Society Second Exhibition, ***Modern Miscellany* (*Shidai*)**, 1933", with eleven paintings in many European modern styles.

### Ni Yide 倪貽德 (1901–70) — the Japan-trained member

- "**Studied in Japan twice.**"
  - First time: "returned to China in **1922**"; "taught at **Shanghai Art Academy**".
  - Second time: "**exposed to more avant-garde European trends**"; "returned to China in **1928**"; "taught at multiple art schools, including Shanghai Art Academy".
- Aim: "**To remake the Chinese art world.**"

***Summer* (1932)** ★
- **What you see:** a young woman sits sideways on a stool in a pink qipao with white trim, holding an open black fan. Her skin is flat yellow-ochre with dark outlines. Shapes are rounded and simplified.
- The slide prints the manifesto line beside it: "we will create a world interwoven with **color, line, and form**".
- **The key comparison (slide 11):** *Summer* beside **Xu Beihong's *Tian Heng and His 500 Retainers*** (1928–30).

| | Ni Yide, *Summer* | Xu Beihong, *Tian Heng* |
|---|---|---|
| Look | Flat colour, simple shapes, visible outlines | Realistic bodies, light and shadow, depth |
| Subject | An ordinary woman sitting | A heroic story from history |
| Aim | The picture itself: colour, line, form | A story with a national message |

**In plain words:** this is the Storm Society's whole argument in one slide. Xu's academic realism (Week 5) is the kind of "representationalism" they wanted to wash away.

***Moonlit Night* (1945)** — a night landscape in loose, broad strokes of deep blue: a dark hill, a small moon, a pale road curving beside water. The same manifesto line appears beside it.

### Pang Xunqin 龐薰琹 (1906–85) — the Paris-trained member

- "Born to a **prosperous family in Jiangsu** province."
- "Initially studied **medicine** at a French-language university in Shanghai."
- "Left Shanghai for studying art in **Paris at 19**."
- "**Returned in 1930**": "taught at a few art academies"; "opened a private art workshop"; "**designed advertisements and book covers**".
- Shown: *Self-portrait* (1929), in a wide-brimmed hat and bow tie.

**His three works — each one teaches a different idea** ★★

| Work | European idea it is paired with | What the slide says it means |
|---|---|---|
| ***Such is Paris*** (1931) | **Collage** — "fragmentation, juxtaposition". Compared with **Pablo Picasso**, *Bowl with Fruit, Violin, and Wineglass* (1913) | "**Fragmented images of materialistic urban life.**" "**Overwhelming emphasis on money in Shanghai** after his return from Paris." |
| ***Composition*** (1934, **destroyed**) | **Surrealism** — "alternative realities; dream worlds or hidden psychological tensions; ideas and images from the unconscious minds". Compared with **Salvador Dalí**, *The Persistence of Memory* (1931) | "**Figures of a robot and a Chinese peasant girl.**" "Images of **industrial machinery**." "**Connected in a dehumanizing bondage.**" |
| ***Son of the Earth*** (1934), watercolour on paper | — | "**A dying child from a peasant family in Jiangnan suffering from a devastating drought**", "accompanied by his parents". "**Started to have concerns for the hardship of Chinese people at that time.**" |

**What you see in each:**

- ***Such is Paris*** — no single scene. Overlapping pieces: several faces (a pale woman with red lips, a man in a top hat, a man in a cap with a moustache), playing cards, a street lamp, and bits of painted words like "HOT" and "CAF". It is painted, but it looks like cut-and-pasted scraps, which is why it is compared with Picasso's collage.
  - A second slide puts it beside ***Such is Shanghai*** (1934): the same overlapping-fragments idea, now with Chinese faces, an opera-style painted face and playing cards.
  - **Careful:** the painting is *called* Paris, but the slide's comment is about **money in Shanghai** after he came home.
- ***Composition*** — a huge grey machine press fills the picture. At the bottom left a metal **robot head** is pressed against the face of a tired young woman in a patterned headscarf (**the peasant girl**). Giant fingers push the machine's lever from above.
  - **In plain words:** machines and people are chained together, and the machine is winning. Modern industry takes away people's humanity.
- ***Son of the Earth*** — a father in a straw hat holds a limp, shirtless boy across his lap; the mother bends towards them with her cheek on her hand. Calm, smooth, simplified figures in blues and greens.
  - **In plain words:** this is the turning point. A painter who started with pure modern form begins to care about the suffering of ordinary Chinese people.

**The order matters:** urban fragments (1931) → machine and human (1934) → a dying peasant child (1934). Pang moves from **form** towards **social concern**.

## Part 4 — The woodcut print (slides 23–26) ★★

### How it works
- "**A printmaking technique.**"
- "**Printing an image from a carved wooden plate.**"
- "Tools: **knives, chisels, gouges (instead of a brush)**."
- "**Raised areas: inked and printed.**"
- "**Recessed areas: do not receive ink, appear blank** on the printed paper."

**In plain words:** it works like a rubber stamp. What you leave standing prints black. What you cut away stays white. The print comes out as a mirror image of the block.

### Its characteristics ★★

| Characteristic | Slide wording |
|---|---|
| **Visual power** | "Sharp contrast of the black ink and uninked paper" |
| **Reproducible** | "Public service" |
| **Propagation of ideas** | — |
| **Less expensive** | "Compared to oil painting, good for poor young artists" |

And: "**An international form of art in early 20th century.**" (Illustrated by a German-language and a Spanish-language political print.)

### International woodcut: Käthe Kollwitz ★
- "Pursued by the **left-wing artists; young people, progressive**."
- Purpose: "**To reveal the oppressive social reality**":
  - "the unspeakably difficult years of **WWI**"
  - "tragedies and aftermath"
  - "**the underrepresented groups, whose presence and demands had not been acknowledged**"
- **Käthe Kollwitz, *War Series*** (1921–23):
  - ***The Volunteers*** — a row of young men surging forward, heads thrown back, led by a figure with a skull-like face.
  - ***The Widow I*** — one woman alone, head bowed, big hands crossed over her body.
  - ***The Mothers*** — women pressed into one tight dark block, arms round each other, children peering out.
  - Slide: "**Left-behind subjects (mothers, widows, and children) and their sorrows.**"

**In plain words:** Kollwitz showed war from the side of the people left behind. Chinese woodcut artists took both her medium and her subject: the people no one else was showing.

## Part 5 — The Modern Woodcut Movement in China (1930s) (slides 27–36) ★★

### Lu Xun (1881–1936), the leader
- The movement was "**introduced and propelled by Lu Xun**".
- "Initially trained in **Japan**, **switched from practical science and medical studies to literary studies** (spiritually liberating)."
  - → "**The father of modern Chinese literature**".
- "Later developed an interest in **left-wing art (woodcut print)** and **political theory**."
  - → "**The father of modern woodcut movement in China**".

**Remember:** Lu Xun was a **writer**, not a woodcut artist. He introduced and promoted the movement. He holds **two "father of" titles**.

### How the movement spread
- "**No manifesto** (not fully defined itself)." ← contrast with the Storm Society
- "**Started in Shanghai.**"
- "Rapidly developed in major cities across the nation": **Hangzhou, Beijing, and Guangzhou**.
- "**Guangzhou: a late epicenter.**"
- "Some woodcut artists **originally trained as oil painting in art academies**."
- Example on the slide: **Lai Shaoqi** (b. 1915), ***Breaking Out!*** (1936), "**woodcut for journal cover**" — angular figures, one thrusting a fist straight up, barbed wire below.

### Its powers, and the price
- **Powers:**
  - "**Raw, easy to read, powerful images**"
  - "**Potential to enlighten the people**"
  - "**Fast and inexpensive to reproduce**"
  - "→ **an excellent medium of social activism**"
- **Used by the left-wing**, so artists were:
  - "**Arrested and charged by the Nationalist government with 'endangering the Republic'**"
  - "**Sentenced to imprisonment**"

### The four works ★★

**1. Jiang Feng, *Kill the Resisters* (1931), woodcut**

| Group | Where | Slide wording |
|---|---|---|
| **Nationalist government troops** | Upper left, under a small white sun emblem | "**Aiming rifles (accuracy) at the crowd**" |
| **Demonstrators** | Lower right, a dense mass of heads | "**Terrified**"; "**Fleeing in chaos**" |

- The slide adds a picture of the Nationalist flag (white sun on blue) and of a rifle, to show how to read the rough details.
- **In plain words:** the government is shooting its own people. The white sun tells you who is shooting.

**2. Jiang Feng, *Workers on the Wharf* (1932), woodcut**
- **What you see:** a line of thin, bowed dock workers walking to the right with heavy poles and cargo hooks; behind them a big steamship, factory chimneys and cranes.
- **Compared with:** **Käthe Kollwitz, *Weavers on the March*** (1897, etching): the same procession of worn-out workers moving in one direction with tools on their shoulders.

**3. Zhang Wang, *Head Wound* (1934), woodcut**
- "**Depicts a man who has head and eye injuries.**"
- "**A social activist.**"
- "**Police brutality against a social activist.**"
- **What you see:** a close-up head, bandage round the forehead and over one eye; the other eye stares out.

**4. Li Hua, *China, Roar!* (1936)** ★★ (the lecture's cover image)
- Li Hua: "**an art teacher in Guangzhou**".
- Theme: "**National awakening and international solidarity.**"
- **Her visual analysis, point by point:**

| What you see | Slide wording |
|---|---|
| The figure | "A man: **in agony; desperate fight**" |
| His state | "**Naked and blindfolded** man" |
| His mouth | "**Wide-open mouth**; left to his voice to reach out to the audience; a strong expression of **anger, outcry**" |
| His pose | "Raised shoulder, crunched arms, and bent legs. **Pyramidal form**" |
| The lines | "**Sharp and angulated lines**, full of animation" |
| The effect | "Charged with force, **electrified body**" |
| His identity | "**Stripped of national identity → universally appealing**" |

- Also in the picture: he is **tied to a post with ropes**, and one hand reaches down for **a dagger on the ground**.
- **In plain words:** he cannot see and cannot move, so all he has is his voice. He is naked, with no clothes to mark him as Chinese, so anyone anywhere can feel for him. That is how a picture titled "China" speaks to the whole world ("international solidarity"). The dagger within reach hints he is about to fight back ("national awakening").

## Conclusion (slides 37–39) ★★

**The avant-garde**
- "**Standing at the forefront to propel changes.**"
- "**Resisting a stale representationalism**, though different targets and contexts."
- "**Multiple origins and operations** in modern China."

**The matched pair:**

| | The Storm Society | The Modern Woodcut Movement |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest** | "The spirit of the modern age; **westernized**" | "Modern life as **a collective struggle for a better future**" |
| **Approach** | "Creating a **pure formal** world"; "**Liberating lines, colors and shapes**" | "**Representing the underrepresented groups**, whose presence and demands had not been acknowledged" |

***Zeitgeist***
- "**The spirit of an era.**"
- "**Social reality or the truth about the current age.**"

**In plain words:** both groups wanted art that fits the modern age. The Storm Society found the modern age in **new forms**. The woodcut artists found it in **the real struggles of ordinary people**.

## Easy to mix up

| Do not confuse | With |
|---|---|
| **Storm Society**: oil, Shanghai, **has a manifesto**, art for art's sake | **Woodcut movement**: prints, four cities, **no manifesto**, social activism |
| **Ni Yide**: trained in **Japan** (twice); *Summer* | **Pang Xunqin**: trained in **Paris**; *Such is Paris* |
| ***Such is Paris*** → **collage** → **Picasso** | ***Composition*** → **Surrealism** → **Dalí** |
| *Such is Paris* is named after Paris | The slide's comment is about **money in Shanghai** |
| **Lu Xun**: a writer who led the woodcut movement; "father" of modern literature **and** of the modern woodcut movement | **Li Hua, Jiang Feng, Zhang Wang**: the artists who cut the prints |
| **Jiang Feng**: *Kill the Resisters*, *Workers on the Wharf* | **Zhang Wang**: *Head Wound*. **Li Hua**: *China, Roar!* |
| *Kill the Resisters*: **troops shoot demonstrators** | *Head Wound*: **police brutality** against one activist |
| **Käthe Kollwitz**: German; *War Series*; mothers, widows, children | **Emil Nolde** (Week 5): German Expressionist painter paired with Lin Fengmian |
| Storm Society "**liberating lines, colors and shapes**" (a group, Week 6) | Lin Fengmian "**liberation of forms, color, and lines**" (one painter, Week 5) — same idea, different people |
| Storm Society "**art for art's sake**" | Xu Beihong "**art for social and political sake**" (Week 5) — and *Summer* is set against his *Tian Heng* |
| **Raised** areas print | **Recessed** (cut-away) areas stay blank |
| **Guangzhou** = the woodcut movement's "late epicenter"; Li Hua taught there | **Shanghai** = where both groups started |
