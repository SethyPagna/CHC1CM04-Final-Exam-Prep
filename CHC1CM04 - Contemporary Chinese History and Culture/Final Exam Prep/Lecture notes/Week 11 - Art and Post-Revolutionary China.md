## The lecture in one minute

- **Mao died in 1976.** The Cultural Revolution ended with him. By **1978 Deng Xiaoping** was in charge.
- Deng dropped Mao's endless political campaigns and put **the economy first**. He **opened China's door** to the outside world.
- Artists had been trained to glorify Mao, the nation and the working class. Now they asked: **what is art for?** They began to **test how much freedom the state would allow**.
- The lecture shows three groups doing this in **1979–1981**:

| Group | Who | Example | What the test showed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Official art** | Artists hired by the state | Beijing airport murals | A modern style was allowed; **nudity was not** |
| **Unofficial art** | Underground artists | The **Stars Group** | Shut down by police, then **allowed after a protest** |
| **Academically trained oil painters** | Realist painters from the academies | *Father*, *Molten Steel & Sweat* | Realism could now show **hardship instead of glory** |

## Words you need

| Word | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| **Post-revolutionary China** | China after Mao's death (1976) and Deng's rise (1978), when "continuous revolution" ended |
| **Class struggle** | Mao's idea that working people must keep fighting other classes to make everyone equal |
| **Open Door Policy** | Deng's policy of letting foreign business, money and people into China |
| **Depoliticization** | Culture and intellectual life stop being run by political campaigns |
| **Rightist** | A label for someone judged to be against the Party's line; it made you a political enemy |
| **Purged** | Removed from your post |
| **Exonerated / rehabilitated** | The label is taken away; your name, job and status are given back |
| **Sent-down youth** | City students sent to the countryside to do farm work during the Cultural Revolution |
| **Official art** | Art that the state approves and pays for |
| **Unofficial art** | Art that the state did not commission or approve |
| **Mural** | A painting on a wall |
| **Nude** | A picture of an unclothed human body; a long-established subject in European art |
| **Cult of personality** | Treating a political leader like a god |
| **Satire** | Making fun of someone or something in order to criticise it |
| **New Realism / Scar Art** | Post-Mao realist painting that shows real suffering and hard lives instead of glorifying them |

## Part 1 — What "post-revolutionary China" means (slides 2–4)

### The political change ★

**Slide wording:** "Defines the shift that began after **Mao Zedong's death in 1976** and **Deng Xiaoping's rise to power in 1978**."

In between, Mao's named successor **Hua Guofeng** held power briefly and was then replaced by Deng.

| | Under Mao | Under Deng |
|---|---|---|
| **Priority** | "Ongoing **class struggle** and **ideological purity**" | "**Economic modernization and stability**" |
| **Method** | One mass political campaign after another | The Party "**officially abandoned mass political campaigns**" |
| **Outside world** | Largely closed, except to socialist countries | "**Open Door Policy**; China's economy **reconnected to the capitalist world**" |

The slide's two headings for this are **"End of Continuous Revolution"** and **"China's Integration into Global Capitalism"**. The pictures are two posters of Deng: one with an oil rig at sea, one with a skyline of glass towers.

### The social change ★

Heading: **"Cultural and Intellectual Depoliticization."**

**1. Rightists and other political prisoners**
- "**Exonerated, returned to positions of influence.**"
- In plain words: people who had been purged and sent away for "re-education" got their names cleared and came back to their jobs in the cities.

**2. Educated urban youths**
- These were city students "formerly sent down to the countryside, engaging in manual labor".
- The slide shows a **1970 poster**: smiling young people lean from a train leaving Shanghai, holding up Little Red Books. Its slogan: **"Go to the countryside, go to the border areas, go to the regions where the motherland needs you the most."**
- Now they "**returned to urban academic circles**".
- ★ "**College admissions examination resumed in 1977**, opened to anyone **younger than 31** and **not on a political blacklist**."
- In plain words: the national university entrance exam had been stopped for about ten years. In 1977 it started again, so a whole generation could finally go to university.

### How artists reacted ★★

The question she asked: artists were skilled at painting and sculpture, and had been told to use those skills to glorify the nation, Mao and the proletariat. After Mao, **should art still serve politics, or should artists say what they themselves think?**

The slide's four **"Artistic reactions"** — learn all four:

1. "**Criticized Mao's policies** of the previous decade"
2. "**Overthrew socialist realism**"
3. "**Retold their vivid mental images of what they had actually seen**" — the struggle sessions, the trauma, the loss and frustration of the Cultural Revolution
4. "**Pushed the freedom of artistic expression**" — testing the limit of what the state would allow

## Part 2 — Official art: the Beijing airport murals (slides 5–22)

### What "official art" is ★
- Art that is **officially approved and sponsored by the state**.
- The artists are paid by the state, usually because they work in **state-owned cultural institutions**, for example as professors at an art academy. The state invites them to take part in its projects.

### The airport ★

| Terminal | Opened |
|---|---|
| **Terminal 1** | **1 January 1980** |
| Terminal 2 | 1 November 1999 |
| Terminal 3 | 2008 (the year of the Beijing Olympics) |

- Terminal 1 was "**opened to celebrate the 30th anniversary of the PRC**" (founded 1949, so the 30th anniversary fell in 1979).
- It was a **public art project**. Around the world at that time, new state buildings were given specially made works by local artists.
- **Two purposes:** to **decorate** large empty walls, and to let the nation **shape and project an image of itself** to the thousands of travellers passing through.
- A photo on the slide shows **Deng Xiaoping** and other leaders visiting the terminal and looking at a mural.
- The slide asks: **"What themes and contents?"** In class she asked what you would paint. The two answers below are the ones to know.

### Mural 1 — Zhang Ding, *Nezha Conquers the Dragon King* (1979) ★

**The artist: Zhang Ding (1917–2010)**
- "Chinese **painter, cartoonist, muralist, calligrapher, arts and crafts artist**."
- "**Professor at the Central Academy of Art and Design, Beijing.**"
- "**Purged in 1957 as a Rightist, and again during the Cultural Revolution; in 1978 he was rehabilitated.**"
- "**Artistic director of *Nezha Conquers the Dragon King* (1979)**" — the film.
- In plain words: a man punished twice under Mao was, once rehabilitated, trusted with a national commission. He is a living example of Part 1.

**The story it comes from**
- *Nezha Conquers the Dragon King* (1979) is "**a Chinese animated fantasy film**", "produced by **Shanghai Animation Film Studio**".
- It adapts "a story in **Chinese mythology** surrounding Nezha".
- **Nezha** is "**a warrior deity**" — a god-like fighter — with a "**magical birth after a gestation period of 3.5 years**" (his mother carried him for three and a half years).
- He **saves his friends**, who were caught by the **East Sea Dragon King**, who is "cruel and destructive, plaguing China with destructive storms and a drought".
- The film was "**internationally screened and released, e.g. BBC Two, 1984**".

**What the mural looks like**
- A very **long horizontal wall painting**.
- **Centre:** the child Nezha inside a swirling disc ringed by **four dragons**, all inside a **thick red circle**. (That red circle is what you see behind Deng in the photo.)
- **Left and right:** mountains, pagodas, palaces, warriors and demons. **Along the bottom:** curling green-and-white waves.
- **Nezha appears several times** across the wall. Zhang Ding took episodes from the film and laid them side by side. In her words, he turned **a time-based film into a space-based mural**.

**The slide's two-line analysis ★★**
- "**Subject matter: national.**"
- "**Form: vivid colors, decorative style; traceable to mural in Dunhuang (national heritage).**"

**In plain words:** both *what* it shows (a Chinese myth) and *how* it is painted (bright, flat, decorative, like the ancient Dunhuang cave murals) say "China". That is why it suited the airport, and why it caused no trouble.

### Mural 2 — Yuan Yunsheng, *Water Splashing Festival* (1979) ★

| | |
|---|---|
| Artist | **Yuan Yunsheng (b. 1937)** |
| Full title | *Water Splashing Festival: Song of Life* |
| Medium and size | "**Mural, acrylic on canvas, 340 × 2,100 cm**" — 21 metres long |
| Where | "**Former dining room, Beijing International Airport**" |

**Subject**
- "**A holiday of the minority Dai people in China's Yunnan.**"
- The Dai are an ethnic minority. At the festival people dress in their finest, colourful clothes, play music and splash water.
- The mural shows a line of tall women dancing, a dancer in a peacock costume, people fetching water among huge tropical leaves. The slides set the painted festival beside photographs of the real one.

**Style ★**
- "**No longer in Soviet socialist realist style.**"
- "But **elongated figural style**, inspired by **early 20th [century] modern European style, esp. Amedeo Modigliani**."
- The slide puts five of the mural's dancers next to a Modigliani portrait: both have **long necks, long oval faces, long narrow bodies and curving outlines**. You hardly see the dancers' eyes.
- She noted the body proportions are still believable; it is the figures that are **stylised**.

**The controversy ★★**
- Two slides are captioned in red: **"Nude — controversial."**
- On the end wall of the dining room is a **bathing scene** with **naked women** washing and pouring water.
- Yuan Yunsheng's thinking: the nude is a normal subject in European and modern art, and people at a water festival would naturally take off their clothes to bathe.
- **The state's reaction:** it "didn't allow the public display of nude scenes". The room was closed and that part of the painting was not allowed to stay on view. (One photo on the slide shows that wall plain white.)

**The lesson she drew ★★**

| What Yuan tried | Allowed? |
|---|---|
| Dropping socialist realism for a **modern European style** | **Yes** |
| Showing **nude** figures | **No** |

"It shows that **freedom was not fully in place**, was not fully allowed, in the post-Mao era."

## Part 3 — Unofficial art: the Stars Group, 1979 (slides 23–34)

### What "unofficial art" is
Works "**not commissioned by the state officially**".

### Who the Stars were ★
"Founded by a group of **Beijing-based underground artists and writers**" who:
- "organized **poetry recitals at unsupervised parks** in Beijing"
- "published **underground journals**"
- "expressed opinion at **Beijing's Democracy Wall**"
- applied to exhibit at the official **Chinese National Art Gallery** — and "**their exhibition… was unapproved**"

Much of their work was abstract, which had not been allowed in the late Mao years, and they were outspoken.

### The exhibition on the fence — 27 September 1979 ★★
- Refused the gallery, they held an **unofficial exhibition right outside it**: works "**hung on the fence and from the trees outside the official Chinese National Art Gallery**".
- "**More than 150 works by 23 artists**: woodcuts, oils, pencil drawings, *guohua*, and wooden sculptures."
- "**Handmade** exhibition posters, object labels, exhibition tickets, and exhibitor's badges." It was unofficial, but organised like a proper show.
- 1979 was both the PRC's 30th anniversary year and one year after China reopened.
- The photos show crowds of ordinary people filing along the fence, looking closely and smiling.

### Wang Keping (b. 1949), "a wood sculptor" ★

His method: he uses the **natural shape of the tree** — the trunk, a branch stub, a bend in the wood — and carves the face out of what is already there.

**1. *Idol* (1979)** — wood, 57 × 30 × 15 cm ★★
- **What you see:** a block of wood carved as one big face. Puffy cheeks, a small closed mouth, a cap with a **five-pointed star**. **The two eyes are different**: one wider open, one nearly shut. The face is squarish because he used the whole block of the trunk.
- **The slide headed "Iconography"** places the sculpture between its two sources:
  - on the left, **two photographs of Mao** (young, in a cap with a red star; older, in a Mao suit);
  - on the right, a **stone Buddha** with a calm face and closed eyes.
- So the sculpture **mixes a political idol with a religious idol**. The cap and star come from Mao; the calm face and curved brows come from Buddhist statues.
- **The slide's interpretation — learn this line:** "**Post-revolutionary satire of Mao as the Communist Star and political idol. Critique of the cult of personality.**"
- The title is itself a question: what kind of idol is this — popular, religious or political?
- The uneven eyes: she suggested "one eye open, one eye closed" may criticise policies that were not transparent or were applied selectively. She left it as a question for the viewer.

**2. *Silence* (1979)** — wood, height 45 cm ★
- **What you see:** a head carved from a log. **One eye is covered by a patch with a crossed grid.** Where the mouth should be, a **branch stub** sticks out, carved with a ring so it looks like **a plug stuffed into the mouth**.
- **What it means:** people **could not see clearly** and **could not speak**. Under Mao, people had thoughts but could not say them freely, and had to pretend not to see what they saw.

**3. *Chain* (1979)** — wood carving, height 53 cm ★
- **What you see:** carved from a branch with a bend in it; the bend becomes the nose. **A hand is clamped over the mouth. A chain is carved around the neck.**
- **What it means:** again people cannot speak, and **their lives are under control**.

Subjects this sensitive are one reason the show had not been approved.

### What happened next ★★ (slide: "Mixed reactions")

| Day | Date | Event |
|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | 27 Sept | "**Rehabilitated senior art administrators appreciated the artists' courage and ambition.**" Staff from the gallery came out to look |
| **Day 2** | 28 Sept | "**Police intervention**, questioning the legality of the artists' activity" |
| **Day 3** | 29 Sept | "**Police sealed off and confiscated the works**" |
| **Day 5** | **1 October, National Day** | "**March for protest; 700 artists and their supporters. Started from Democracy Wall toward Tiananmen Square**" |

- The banner in the photograph reads "march to uphold the constitution"; placards read "**art demands freedom**". The marchers argued the constitution gave them the right to demonstrate.
- **Result:** "**officially approved and reopened at Beihai Park in November**."
- And: "**A second Stars show at the National Art Gallery a year later**" — inside the very gallery that had refused them.

**In plain words:** the state first said no, then the police shut the show, then after a public protest on National Day the state gave way. The boundary could be pushed.

## Part 4 — Academically trained oil painters: New Realism / Scar Art (slides 35–43)

### The idea ★★
- These painters were **well trained in oil painting** and **very good at realistic depiction**.
- Their pictures still look real — but they "**no longer fall into the socialist realism**" demanded under Mao.
- In her words, they "**turned realism upside down**". They "no longer use the painting technique to glorify the state policy… but they tell you the real life" of workers and farmers.
- They stopped being part of the "**propaganda machine**".

Each painting is taught by putting it beside a Mao-era poster of the same subject.

### Luo Zhongli, *Father* (1980) ★★

| | |
|---|---|
| Medium and size | "**Oil on canvas, 227 × 154 cm**" — over two metres tall, **larger than life** |
| Subject | The face of an old **peasant** (farmer) |

**What you see**
- A huge **frontal** face looking straight at you, filling the canvas.
- **Very dark, sun-browned skin** covered in **deep wrinkles**; beads of sweat.
- A cloth wound round his head.
- **Rough fingers with dirt around the nails**; dry, cracked skin.
- He holds **a bowl of water**. Behind him is a golden field on a sunny day. He seems to be taking a short break from hard work.
- The slide also zooms in on a detail: a **ballpoint pen tucked behind his ear**.

**The effect:** standing in front of a head that size is "very striking".

**Compared with the 1965 poster** *"The poor and lower-middle peasants are the masters of the people's commune"* (Shanghai People's Art Publishing, April 1965):

| | 1965 poster | *Father*, 1980 |
|---|---|---|
| Age and looks | Younger, sturdy, handsome | Old, worn |
| Face | Smiling, few wrinkles | Deeply wrinkled, unsmiling |
| Hands | Smooth | Dry, cracked, dirty nails |
| What he is doing | Pouring out a **big harvest of grain** | Pausing with a **bowl of water** |
| Message | The peasant is the proud **master** of the commune; farming is an honourable job | **A farmer's life is harsh** |

### Guang Tingbo, *Molten Steel & Sweat* (1981) ★★

**The artist: Guang Tingbo (born 1938, Dalian, Liaoning Province)**
- "**An oil painter of Manchu ethnicity.**"
- "Graduated from the **Oil Painting Department of the Lu Xun Fine Arts Academy in 1964**" — just before the Cultural Revolution.
- Worked in the **Liaoning Branch of the Chinese Artists Association**; moved to the Liaoning painting academy in 1980 and later headed its creative department.

**What you see**
- A young **steelworker** in a white hard hat, sitting and facing us, holding a **white enamel mug** of water.
- He is **drenched in sweat**: it runs down his face and neck, and his vest is soaked. His jacket is pulled down off his shoulders.
- His face is tired and far-away — "disoriented", she said.
- Behind him, other workers have stripped off their shirts; one tips his head back to drink.

**The point:** working in a steel plant is **not as comfortable or honourable as people imagined** in the Mao era. It is hot, exhausting work.

**Compared with two posters:**

| | "We and ore together make steel!" (Jan 1959) | Anshan Iron and Steel poster (April 1977) | *Molten Steel & Sweat* (1981) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who | Two university students | Factory workers | A steelworker |
| Pose | Standing tall, arm raised, holding up the metal | Standing high, waving a flag | Sitting, resting |
| Face | Determined, healthy, **no sweat** | Young, confident, full of energy | Tired, sweating, dazed |
| Message | Making steel is **honourable and heroic**; everyone enjoys joining in | Production is a glorious revolutionary cause | Making steel is **hard, harsh work** |

- The 1977 poster's slogan: *"Hold high the great red banner of the 'Charter of the Anshan Iron and Steel Company', to set off the high tide of grasping revolution and promoting production."* It dates from **after Mao's death but before the Open Door Policy**, so it still glorifies.

**The key sentence for both comparisons ★★:** **both the posters and the paintings use realism.** Socialist realism had to "infuse a sense of positive thinking, optimism" into the picture. Post-Mao painters used the same skill to "tell you the true story".

## The conclusion

The "Conclusion" slide has **no words** — only three pictures: the *Nezha* mural, *Molten Steel & Sweat*, and Wang Keping's *Silence*. They stand for the three parts of the lecture: **official art, academic oil painting, unofficial art**.

What she said aloud:
- The lecture showed "**a series of reactions by post-Mao era artists**", whether they worked **inside the academy system or outside it**.
- "**Both groups of artists… test the limit of what the state allowed them to do.**"
- Some **continued to use realism**, the technique they had been trained in.
- **Underground artists** "employed **abstraction**" and used their skills to show what people went through under Mao.
- This was the art scene of the **transitional period from the Mao to the post-Mao era**.
