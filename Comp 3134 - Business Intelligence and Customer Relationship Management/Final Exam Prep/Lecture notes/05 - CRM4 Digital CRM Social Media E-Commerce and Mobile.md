## The lecture in one minute

- Marketing communications are how firms "inform, persuade and remind customers". Customers are now fragmented in time and space, so firms add the Internet, mobile devices and social media to the 8 traditional tools.
- Social media is "user-generated" content delivered on Web 2.0 tools. The customer takes part actively: share, create, talk, work, buy/sell, learn.
- Web 3.0 is the smarter web: 9 expected capabilities and 5 concerns.
- Social media commerce changes business (12 areas, 10 changes). People meet in 6 types of virtual communities. Social networking sites offer 16 capabilities. Firms use them in 10 ways.
- ★★ Digital disruption: "Offer new value to customers, threatening and invalidating existing business models." There are 9 business models and 5 kinds of disruption.
- Sharing economy: "Collaborative consumption, peer economy" (ride sharing, accommodation sharing).
- M-commerce has 5 named attributes: ubiquity, convenience, interactivity, personalization, localization. It has 11 applications and 10 benefits for customers.
- Location-based m-commerce: 5 functions, a set of components, a 5-step process, 7 applications, 4 barriers. M-commerce also has security and privacy issues and technological barriers.

### Slide 2 — Communications to customers ★ (definition; prime MCQ / true-false material)

- "Marketing communications are the means by which firms attempt to inform, persuade and remind customers – directly or indirectly – about the brands they market."
- "It represents the 'voice' of the company and its brands, and are the ways in which it can establish a dialogue and build relationships with customers."
- "Fragmentation (Time and Space) of target customers." Under this point the slide lists:
  1. "Communication Technology and the Internet."
  2. "Mobile and portable tablets and smartphones."
  3. "Social Media."

**In plain words:** Marketing communications are every way a company talks to its customers. Remember three verbs: inform (tell), persuade (convince), remind (keep the brand in mind). Remember two aims: "establish a dialogue" and "build relationships". Today customers are split up in time and place. So the company must reach them through the Internet, mobile devices and social media.

**In plain words:** Three terms look alike. Social network = the relationships (a thing). Social networking services = the provider of the tools. Also: Web 2.0 is the tool, social media is the content made with the tool.

## Part 4 — Digital disruption and the sharing economy (slides 21–25)

### Slide 21 — Social Media - Digital Disruption ★★ (asked in the mid-term past paper, MCQ Question 7: Ecosystem)

Definition on the slide: "Offer new value to customers, threatening and invalidating existing business models."

**In plain words:** A firm uses digital technology to give customers something new. Old firms then lose their way of earning money.

| # | Model | Slide examples | Slide mechanism | How to tell it apart (key phrase) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Subscription Model | Netflix, Apple Music | "'lock-in' by repeat charging a subscription." | lock-in by repeat charging |
| 2 | Free Model and Freemium Model | Free: Google, Facebook. Freemium: Spotify, LinkedIn, Dropbox | "selling personal data or 'advertising eyeballs'." | selling personal data; advertising eyeballs |
| 3 | Marketplace Model | eBay, iTunes, App Store, Uber, AirBnB | "a digital marketplace that brings together buyers and sellers directly." | brings buyers and sellers together |
| 4 | Access-over-Ownership Model | Zipcar, Peerbuy, AirBnB | "monetising their assets (home, car, capital) by lending them to 'borrowers'." | lending assets to borrowers |
| 5 | Hypermarket Model | Amazon, Apple | "Selling below cost price." | selling below cost |
| 6 | Experience Model | Tesla, Apple | "providing a superior experience." | superior experience |
| 7 | Pyramid Model | Amazon, Microsoft, Dropbox | "recruiting an army of resellers." | army of resellers |
| 8 | On-Demand Model | Uber, Operator, Taskrabbit | "monetising time and selling instant-access. Taking a commission from people with money but no time who pay for goods and services delivered or fulfilled by people with time but no money." | monetising time; instant access; commission |
| 9 | Ecosystem Model | Apple, Google | "selling an interlocking and interdependent suite of products and services that increase in value as more are purchased. Creates consumer dependency." | interlocking suite; consumer dependency |

**In plain words, one line each:**
1. Subscription: you pay again and again, so you stay.
2. Free / Freemium: the user pays nothing; the firm earns from data and adverts. **(extra, not on the slides)** Freemium means the basic version is free and a better version is paid.
3. Marketplace: the firm is the meeting place for buyers and sellers.
4. Access-over-Ownership: you use a thing without buying it; the owner earns by lending it.
5. Hypermarket: the selling price is lower than the cost.
6. Experience: people choose it because using it feels better.
7. Pyramid: other people sell for the firm.
8. On-Demand: you get it now; busy people with money pay people who have time.
9. Ecosystem: every product works with the others; the more you buy, the more each one is worth, so you depend on the firm.

**Warning: a company name alone does not identify the model.** The same company appears under several models on this slide. Always decide by the mechanism phrase.

## Part 5 — Mobile commerce (slides 26–30)

### Slide 26 — The Attributes of M-Commerce ★ (usable as examples in a touch-point question like 2025-26 final Q25(c); prime true-false list)

As numbered on the slide:

1. "Ubiquity. everywhere, especially at the same time."
2. "Almost all mobile phones are smartphones, we have easier ubiquity."
3. "Convenience and capabilities. The functionality and usability of mobile devices are increasing, while their physical size remains small and the cost is affordable."
4. "Interactivity. Mobile systems allow for fast and easy interactions (e.g., via Twitter, tablets, or smartphones)."
5. "Personalization. Mobile devices are personal devices."
6. "Localization. Knowing where a user is physically located in real time provides an opportunity to offer him or her relevant mobile advertisements, coupons, or other services. Such services are known as location-based m-commerce."

**Note on the count:** the slide shows six numbered points, but point 2 only continues ubiquity. There are five named attributes: ubiquity, convenience (and capabilities), interactivity, personalization, localization.

**In plain words:** Ubiquity = the service is everywhere you are. Convenience = the device is small, cheap and can do more and more. Interactivity = fast and easy two-way contact. Personalization = one device belongs to one person, so the message can be for that person. Localization = the firm knows where you are now and can send an offer that fits the place.

### Slides 32–33 — Components of a location-based service

As numbered on the slides:

1. "Location finder (positioning) component." "A GPS (or other device) that finds the location of a person or a thing."
2. "Mobile Positioning Center." "This includes a server that manages the location information received from the location finder."
3. "User." "The user can be a person or thing (e.g., a vehicle)."
4. "Mobile devices." "The user needs a mobile device (e.g., a smartphone) that includes a GPS or other feature that locates the location (position) of something or someone."
5. "Mobile communication network." "The network(s) that transfers user requests to the service providers and then transmits the reply to the user."
6. "Service or application providers." "Providers are responsible for servicing a user's request. They may use applications such as GIS."
7. "Data or content provider." "Service providers usually need to acquire (e.g., geographic, financial, or other data) in order to provide a reply to requests. Data may include maps, coupons, and GIS information."
8. "Geographical information system (GIS)." "This includes maps, location of businesses, and more."
9. "Opt-in application." "In the United States and some other countries, LBS can be used only with people's permission"
10. "(opt-in)." "This requires an additional software app."

**Note on the count:** the slide numbering reaches 10, but number 10 is only the end of point 9, "(opt-in)", pushed onto a new line. The real point is: "LBS can be used only with people's permission (opt-in). This requires an additional software app." So there are nine different components, shown under ten numbers.

**In plain words:** A location-based service needs something that finds the position (1), a server that manages the position data (2), a user (3) with a device (4), a network that carries the request and the reply (5), a provider that answers the request (6), a supplier of data such as maps and coupons (7), a map system (8), and the user's permission (9–10).

### Slide 34 — The location-based service process ★ (5 steps; order can be tested)

1. "The user expresses his or her wish by clicking on a function (e.g., 'find me the nearest gas station')."
2. "The mobile network service finds where the user is located using satellite and GPS."
3. "The request is transferred via a wireless network to the application service provider software that activates a search for the needed data."
4. "The server goes to a database, to find, for example, the nearest requested business and check if it is open, what it serves, and so forth."
5. "Using a GIS, the service delivers the reply to the user, including a map and driving directions if necessary."

**In plain words:** Click → find the user → send the request → search the database → reply with a map. The GIS is used in the last step.
