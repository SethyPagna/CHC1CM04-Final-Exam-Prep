## The lecture in one minute

- Customers are not equally valuable. The Pareto (iceberg) principle: "the top 20 percent of your customers account for 80 percent of your profits". The 150–20 rule is stronger: the best 20% make 150% of the profits and the worst 20% lose 100%.
- So companies use **tiered servicing**: expensive personal service for the profitable few, cheap service for the rest. BI supplies the profit numbers.
- Relationships are "always a share" (easy to switch) or "lost for good" (hard to switch). Companies build "artificial lost for good" relationships.
- Without segmentation there is one offer for all buyers. With segmentation, marketing has four levels: niches, local, segment groups, individual (one-to-one).
- STP = segmentation → targeting → positioning. A positioning map shows brands on two axes (price and quality). There are five reasons for repositioning.
- Customer-perceived value (CPV) = total customer benefit − total customer cost, compared with the alternatives. Delivering it gives a sustainable competitive advantage (SCA). BI analyses CPV in five steps.
- CRM systems and BI help in four ways: acquire the right customer, craft the right value proposition, institute the best processes, motivate employees.
- Six emotions are shown with video examples: fear, humor, animation, sex, music, fantasy and surrealism.

## Part 1 — Is CRM for everyone? The Pareto principle (slides 1–2)

Slide 1 is the title slide ("Lecture CRM.3").

### Slide 2 — "Is CRM for everyone?" ★★

Why ★★: asked as a mid-term MCQ, in mid-term B2(a), and in the 2025-26 final Q25(b).

- Slide wording: "The iceberg, or Pareto, principle says that the top 20 percent of your customers account for 80 percent of your profits."
- **In plain words:** A small group of customers gives most of the profit. So the answer to the slide title is "not equally". A company should spend more CRM effort on the customers who earn it money.
- "Iceberg principle" and "Pareto principle" are the same thing. The marking scheme gives a mark for saying this.

**The picture (cumulative profit curve).** Axes: "Cumulative % of Customers" (across) and "Profits % of Total" (up). Customers are counted from the most profitable to the least profitable.

Slide note: "The final profit is 100%, but when no. of customers reaching at 55%, the profit is 250%. That means last half of the company's customers destroyed value."

How to read the curve:

1. It rises fast at the start. The first customers are very profitable. By about 20% of customers the curve is already above 200%.
2. It is flat at the top, about 250%, around 55% of customers. These customers add almost nothing.
3. It falls back to 100% at the end. The last customers lose money.

**In plain words:** If the company served only its best 55% of customers, its profit would be 2.5 times today's profit. The remaining customers remove 150 points of profit (250 − 100). This picture shows the same idea as the 150–20 rule on slide 22.

## Part 2 — Tiered servicing supported by Business Intelligence (slides 3–5)

### Slide 3 — Tiered servicing ★★

Why ★★: mid-term B2(c) asked for THREE tiered-servicing strategies; mid-term B3(a) (LUX Bank) asked for three tiers; final Q25(b) asked for "differential treatment".

Slide wording: "Companies charge different amounts for different level of servicing."

The full list on the slide:

- "Small-fee clients may only receive infrequent services."
- "Larger-fee clients get frequent and in-person services."
- "Lost-cost [= low-cost] methods of serving 80% of their customers who are low profit."
- "High-cost methods of serving 20% of their customers who are high profit."
- "Companies with a steep value-skewed customer base - VIP status, building strong customer relationships, continuously providing 'surprises' to retain these 'segments' of customers."
- "Companies with shallow value-skewed customer base – where the profits generated by each customer are more or less the same, are better served by reducing costs across the board, perhaps through automation, AI and intelligent systems in order to attract competitors' customers."

**In plain words:** Sort customers by profit. Give the profitable few expensive, personal service. Serve the many low-profit customers in a cheap way. BI does the sorting: it calculates the profit of each customer or segment.

| | Steep value-skewed base | Shallow value-skewed base |
|---|---|---|
| What it means | Profit per customer is very unequal | Profit per customer is "more or less the same" |
| What to do | "VIP status", "building strong customer relationships", "continuously providing 'surprises'" | "reducing costs across the board", "automation, AI and intelligent systems" |
| Goal | "retain" the top segments | "attract competitors' customers" |

The three strategies to write in the exam: (1) different service levels, (2) VIP status programs for a steep base, (3) cost reduction through automation/AI for a shallow base.

Example **(extra, not on the slides)**: a bank gives its richest clients a personal manager, and other clients use the app and the ATM.

### Slide 4 — Multichannel or single-channel value propositions ★

Why ★: "bricks" vs "clicks" is easy MCQ / true-false material.

- "'Bricks' – Stores and Kiosks"
- "'Clicks' – ATMS and the Internet."

The four questions a company must answer (BI answers them with data):

- "Which channel customers use to access their products and services?"
- "Which channel provide customers greatest help in terms of searching, recommendations, experience sharing, trust, personal relationships?"
- "Which channel provides customers greatest rewards?"
- "Which channel provides companies greatest profits?"

Last line: "Managing the multichannel network will be part of the CRM manager's responsibility."

**In plain words:** Bricks = places you walk into. Clicks = electronic self-service. Careful: on the slide a kiosk is "bricks" but an ATM is "clicks".

## Part 3 — Market segmentation and the four levels of marketing (slides 6–13)

### Slide 6 — Without market segmentation ★

Why ★: the examples and the three reasons are easy MCQ material.

- "Mass production, mass distribution and mass marketing communication of one market offering for all buyers."
- "E.g., Coca-Cola, one kind of coke, standing [= standard] packing."
- "Proprietary product, lowest prices and highest profit margins."
- "Supplier dependent marketing and 'value'."
- "Eliminate competition at all costs."
- "E.g., Water supply, Electricity supply, petroleum, telephone lines, cross harbor tunnels and many others"

Three reasons these markets have no segmentation:

1. "For national security reasons."
2. "Geographical limitations."
3. "Huge investments involved."

**In plain words:** One product for everybody. The supplier decides what "value" is, because buyers have little or no choice. Everyone gets the same tap water.

### Slide 7 — With market segmentation ★★

Why ★★: the four levels were tested in a mid-term MCQ ("Which segmentation level is focused on one-to-one marketing?").

- "Number of providers exceeds the customer uptake."
- "Customers have a winder [= wider] choice of suppliers."
- "Customers determine the 'value' of the products/ services."
- "Products and services are broken up to suit customers' preferred standards."
- "Marketing breaks down into four levels": "Niches, Local, Segment Groups and Individual". The picture ("Levels of market segmentation") shows four boxes: Niche, Local, Segment groups, Individual.

**In plain words:** There are more sellers than buyers need. So buyers choose, and buyers decide what is valuable. Sellers must cut the market into groups and fit each group.

### Slide 8 — Market segment and niche market ★

Why ★: not asked yet, but the niche definition is a typical MCQ item.

- "A market segment consists of a group of customers who share a similar set of needs and wants."
- "A niche market is a more narrowly defined customer group seeking a distinctive mix of benefits or values. E.g., Rental cars for business companies, and leisure travelers. Bang & Olufsen for expensive earphones and hi-fi systems."

**In plain words:** A niche is a small part of a segment. Its customers want something special and pay extra for it. It is small in volume but still worth serving.

## Part 4 — Segmentation, targeting, positioning and repositioning (slides 14–16)

### Slide 14 — Creating differentiation and positioning strategies ★

Why ★: STP and the positioning definition are typical MCQ items. The SCA sentence supports the mid-term B4(b) answer.

1. STP: "A company discovers different needs and groups in the marketplace (segmentation). It selects those targets (targeting) it can satisfy in a competitive way and then positions (positioning) its offering, so the target market recognizes the company's distinctive offering and image."
2. SCA: "For market offerings to be successful in current buyers' markets, they need to be developed and differentiated against competitive offerings if a company wants to achieve a sustained competitive advantage (SCA)."
3. Positioning: "Positioning is the successful creation of a customer-focused value proposition, a cogent reason why the target customer should buy from the provider. (e.g., High Technology, Skin and Beauty care, Durability and Safety, Fashion and Lifestyle, …etc.)"

**In plain words:** Segmentation = find the groups. Targeting = choose the groups you can serve better than others. Positioning = make these groups see you as different and worth buying.

### Slide 15 — Positioning map (chocolates) ★★

Why ★★: mid-term B4(c) gave a blank quality × price map and asked students to place Divine, Green & Black's and Kinder (3 marks).

The picture is a cross with two axes. Up–down: "High price" (top) to "Low price" (bottom). Left–right: "Low quality" (left) to "High quality" (right).

| Area of the map | Brands on the slide |
|---|---|
| High price, lower quality (top left) | Cadbury Dairy Milk, Mars Galaxy, Nestlé Yorkie |
| High price, high quality (top right) | Swiss chocolate e.g. Lindt, Belgian chocolate, Green & Black's (nearer the centre) |
| Low price, high quality (bottom right) | Marks & Spencer (just below the middle), Fair trade, Sainsbury's, Tesco (lowest price) |
| Low price, low quality (bottom left) | Empty on the slide |

**In plain words:** A positioning map is a picture of how customers see competing brands on two measures. Brands that sit close together are seen as similar, so they compete most directly **(extra, not on the slides)**.

How to place a brand on a blank map:

1. Label both ends of both axes exactly as the question gives them.
2. For each brand ask two questions. Is its price above or below the middle? Is its quality above or below the middle?
3. Put a dot in that area, write the brand name, and add a short reason.

### Slide 16 — Repositioning ★

Why ★: not asked yet, but a numbered five-item list is typical short-question material.

1. "A competitor launches a market offering that is as good as or better than our existing products. E.g., Electric cars."
2. "Consumer changes their preferences. Heathy [= healthy] food, vegetarian diets,"
3. "New products, AI systems."
4. "Company positioning error. Initial miss calculation, and wrong estimation, over optimistic, problematic leadership, environmental and unexpected events and circumstances."
5. "Re-positioning the company for better survival, appear [= appeal] to younger generations, 'sport' like luxury products."

The slide picture is a street-food stall (no text).

**In plain words:** Repositioning means changing how customers see your offering. Reasons 1–3 come from outside (competitor, consumer, new products). Reasons 4–5 come from the company (its own mistake, its wish to survive and reach younger buyers).

## Part 5 — The customer-oriented organisation and customer-perceived value (slides 17–21)

### Slide 17 — Traditional versus modern customer-oriented organization

### Slide 18 — Customer perceived value ★★

Why ★★: mid-term B4(b) asked about CPV and SCA (2 marks); CPV definitions are prime MCQ material.

1. "Customer-perceived value is the difference between the prospective customer's evaluation of all the benefits and all the costs of a company's offering and the perceived alternatives."
2. "Total customer benefit is the perceived monetary value of the bundle of economic, functional and psychological benefits that customers expect from a given market offering because of the products, services, personnel and image involved."

The diagram:

| Total customer benefit (what the customer gets) | Total customer cost (what the customer gives up) |
|---|---|
| Product benefit | Monetary cost |
| Services benefit | Time cost |
| Personnel benefit | Energy cost |
| Image benefit | Psychological cost |

**In plain words:** CPV = total customer benefit − total customer cost, as the customer sees it, compared with other choices. "Perceived" means the customer's opinion counts, not the company's.

Example **(extra, not on the slides)**: you choose between two restaurants. Benefits: the food (product), table service (services), friendly staff (personnel), a famous name (image). Costs: the bill (monetary), the queue (time), the walk there (energy), worry about a bad meal (psychological).

### Slide 19 — The pursuit of customer-perceived value ★★

Why ★★: supports the mid-term B4(b) answer on CPV and SCA; E1 / E2 / SCA is prime MCQ material.

- "E1 – Efficiency / Least-cost production oriented"
- "E2 – Effectiveness / Customer Perceived Value market orientation"
- "SCA – Sustainable competitive advantage"

The diagram, left to right: **Firm** ↔ (E1, E2) ↔ an oval called "Pursuit of CPV" → "CPV Delivery" → **SCA**. Inside the oval are seven items (the 7 Ps): Product, Price, Place, Promotion, People, Process, Physical evidence.

Labels on the outer arrows: "Company approach", "Knowledge of market place and competitive offerings", "Understanding competition".

**In plain words:** E1 looks inside: make it cheaply. E2 looks outside: give customers what they value. The firm uses both, through the 7 Ps, to deliver CPV. Delivering CPV better than competitors gives SCA.

### Slide 20 — Business Intelligence for customer-perceived value analysis ★★

Why ★★: mid-term B4(a) asked for the FIVE steps, 1 mark each.

1. "Customers are asked, data are collected to analyze what attributes and performance levels they look for in choosing a product, and possible providers."
2. "Classifying and Clustering quantitatively the importance of the attributes and benefits, e.g., how many people have selected what attributes, and their rankings."
3. "Assess the company's and competitor's performance on different customer value attributes against their rated importance. E.g., through market researches."
4. "Analyze different segmentation of customers, and their relative results."
5. "Be aware of customer values changing over time, and performance [= perform] the analysis periodically."

**In plain words:** (1) Ask customers what matters. (2) Count and rank what matters. (3) Score us and our competitors on those things. (4) Repeat the scores for each segment. (5) Do it again regularly, because values change.

### Slide 21 — Building customer satisfaction ★

Why ★: not asked yet, but the three methods and CPQ / CPP / CPV are typical MCQ / true-false items.

"Measurement Methods (BI needed)":

1. "Periodic surveys, repurchase intention, recommend to others."
2. "Customer complaints."
3. "Following calls, immediate internet questions to find out Customer-perceived quality (CPQ), relative to Customer-perceived price (CPP) defines Customer-perceived value (CPV). E.g.,"
   - "Fitness for use"
   - "Conformance to requirements"
   - "Freedom from choice, and variations"
   - "The purchase attributes that the customer perceives and actual experience in using"
   - "Time and effort involved"

**In plain words:** Three ways to measure satisfaction: ask in surveys, count complaints, and follow up right after the purchase. The follow-up finds the quality the customer feels (CPQ) and compares it with the price the customer feels (CPP). Together they give the value the customer feels (CPV).

## Part 6 — Customer profitability: the 150–20 rule (slide 22)

### Slide 22 — Customer profitability ★★

Why ★★: mid-term B2(b) asked for the 150–20 rule (2 marks).

Slide wording: "The 150–20 rule: the 20 per cent most profitable customers generate as much as 150 per cent of the profits of a company; the 20 per cent least profitable lose 100 per cent of the profits."

### How to do any segment-profit / Pareto question in 5 steps

| Step | What to do | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Profit per customer, for each segment | average revenue − average cost to serve |
| 2 | Segment profit ("profit contribution"), then the total | number of customers × profit per customer; add all segments |
| 3 | Two shares for each segment | segment profit ÷ total profit; segment customers ÷ total customers |
| 4 | Find the top 20% of customers | Rank segments by profit **per customer**, best first. Target = 20% × total customers. Count down the ranking until you reach the target. If the target falls inside a segment, take only the customers you still need. |
| 5 | Compare with Pareto and conclude | Does the top 20% give about 80% of profit? Name the priority segment(s). Justify with profit per customer, retention (lifetime value) and tiered servicing. |

Common mistakes:

- Ranking by total segment profit. "Top 20% of customers" means the best individual customers, so rank by profit per customer.
- Using revenue and forgetting to subtract the cost to serve.
- Forgetting to multiply by the number of customers.
- Mixing "% of customers" with "% of profit".
- Leaving out the currency (HKD) or the check of the total.

## Easy to mix up

| A | B | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Pareto / iceberg principle: top 20% → 80% of profits | 150–20 rule: top 20% → 150% of profits, bottom 20% lose 100% | Pareto has no loss in it. The 150–20 rule has customers who "lose" profit. 80 goes with Pareto; 150 and 100 go with the 150–20 rule. |
| Steep value-skewed base | Shallow value-skewed base | Steep = very unequal profits → VIP status, relationships, surprises, to retain. Shallow = "more or less the same" → cut costs, automation / AI, to attract competitors' customers. |
| Always a share | Lost for good | Always a share = easy switching, transaction marketing. Lost for good = high switching cost, relationship marketing. |
| "Artificial lost for good" | Real lost for good | Artificial is created for always-a-share products, with contracts, pre-payment, year-end discounts, exclusive gifts. |
| Bricks: stores and kiosks | Clicks: ATMs and the Internet | Kiosk = bricks. ATM = clicks. |
| Without segmentation | With segmentation | Without: supplier decides "value", one offer for all. With: customers decide "value", providers exceed customer uptake. |
| Market segment | Niche market | Segment = group with similar needs and wants. Niche = "more narrowly defined", "distinctive mix of benefits", pays a premium, small volume. |
| Local marketing | Individual marketing | Local = an area or neighbourhood. Individual = "segments of one", one-to-one. |
| Customization | Personalization | Customization: "you are active". Personalization: "you are passive". |
| Segmentation | Targeting / Positioning | Discover groups → select groups → make them recognise your distinctive offering and image. |
| Positioning | Repositioning | Positioning creates the value proposition. Repositioning changes it, for one of five reasons. |
| Total customer benefit: product, services, personnel, image | Total customer cost: monetary, time, energy, psychological | "Image" is a benefit. "Psychological" is a cost. |
| E1: efficiency, least-cost production oriented | E2: effectiveness, CPV market orientation | E1 is about cost. E2 is about the customer. |
| CPQ (quality) | CPP (price) | CPQ relative to CPP defines CPV. |
| BI for market segmentation (slide 13): data, cost and profit, potential, positioning, evaluation | BI for CPV analysis (slide 20): five numbered steps | Slide 13 is about choosing segments. Slide 20 is about what customers value. |
| "Cost and Profit" = "Specific segments' statistical analysis reports" | "Potential" = "Segment profitability" | Profitability belongs to "Potential", not to "Cost and Profit". |
| Traditional chart: top management on top | Modern chart: customers on top | The modern chart is the traditional one turned upside down. |

## Memory hooks

- **Pareto:** "20 gives 80". Also called iceberg.
- **150–20:** "top 20 → +150, bottom 20 → −100".
- **Three tiered-servicing strategies:** L-V-A = Levels of service, VIP status (steep), Automation (shallow). "Steep → Stars. Shallow → Save cost."
- **Artificial lost for good (4):** C-P-Y-G = Contracts, Pre-payment, Year-end discounts, Gifts.
- **No segmentation, three reasons:** S-G-I = Security (national), Geography, Investment (huge).
- **Four levels:** N-L-S-I = Niches, Local, Segment groups, Individual. "Nobody Likes Selling Identically."
- **Active or passive:** Customization = the Customer Chooses.
- **BI for market segmentation (5):** D-C-P-P-E = Data, Cost and profit, Potential, Positioning, Evaluation.
- **STP:** discover → select → position.
- **Repositioning (5):** Competitor, Consumer preferences, New products, Company error, Company survival. Three from outside, two from inside.
- **CPV benefits:** P-S-P-I = Product, Services, Personnel, Image. **CPV costs:** M-T-E-P = Monetary, Time, Energy, Psychological.
- **7 Ps:** Product, Price, Place, Promotion, People, Process, Physical evidence.
- **E1 and E2:** E1 = Efficiency = least cost (think "1 = lowest cost"). E2 = Effectiveness = customer value (think "2 = the second party, the customer").
- **Five BI steps for CPV:** Ask → Cluster → Assess → Segment → Repeat.
- **Satisfaction measures (3):** Surveys, Complaints, Follow-up calls. "Quality relative to Price = Value."
- **CRM + BI help (4):** A-C-I-M = Acquiring, Crafting, Instituting, Motivating. Bullets 2-4-4-2.
- **Six emotions:** F-H-A-S-M-F = Fear, Humor, Animation, Sex, Music, Fantasy and surrealism. Fear = SimpliSafe.
- **Calculation:** Per customer → Segment → Shares → Rank to 20% → Compare and justify.
