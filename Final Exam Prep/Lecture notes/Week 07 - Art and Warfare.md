## The lecture in one minute

- The war: **the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945)**.
- China was split into zones. The lecture goes through art made in **three places**, and the whole lecture hangs on this table:

| Zone | Place | Artist | Work | Role of art | Method |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Nationalist-controlled areas** | Chongqing | **Huang Junbi** | *Bombing of Chongqing* (1939), ink | Capture social reality | Realism / **reportage** |
| | Guilin | **Cai Dizhi** | *Fleeing Guilin by the North Station* (1944), woodcut | Capture social reality | Realism |
| | Chongqing | **Ding Cong** | *Images of Today* 現象圖 (1945), ink and gouache | Affect public opinion | **Satire in cartoon** |
| | Dunhuang | **Zhang Daqian** | *Portrait of Guanyin from Dunhuang Fresco* (1943) | Raise morale | **Chinese cultural heritage** |
| **2. Japanese-controlled areas** | Beijing, Shanghai | **Jiang Zhaohe** | *Refugees* (1943), ink and colour | Capture social reality | Realism |
| **3. The international sphere** | USA | **Zhang Shuqi** | *Messengers of World Peace* (1940) | Win political support | **Symbolism** |
| | Singapore, India | **Xu Beihong** | *Four Horses* (1940) | **Generate funds** | **Symbolism** |

- **Three roles of art in wartime:** capture social realities; raise morale and affect public opinion; generate funds to support the war effort.
- **Four methods:** realism; satire in cartoon; deployment of the Chinese cultural heritage; symbolism and allegory.

(The "role" and "method" columns are my matching of her conclusion lists to the works. The two lists themselves are from her slides.)

## Part 2 — Zone 1: the Nationalist-controlled areas (slides 9–34)

"e.g. **Chongqing, Guilin, Dunhuang**"

### 2a. Chongqing — Huang Junbi, *Bombing of Chongqing, June 11, 1939* (1939) ★★

**The artist: Huang Junbi 黄君璧 (1898–1991)**
- "**A *guohua* university professor** who had moved with **the National Central University from Nanjing to Chongqing**."
- "**Close to the Nationalist government. Relocated to Taiwan after 1949.**"

**The event**
- "The **Japanese bombing of Chongqing on June 11, 1939**."
- "He was **visiting a friend on the southern bank of the Jialing River**." So he watched the city burn from across the water.

**What you see:** an ink landscape in the old hanging-scroll format. Huge curling clouds of **smoke** rise from a town on the river and fill the top two-thirds. Pine trees on a cliff and a roofed building sit in the foreground. The view is high and far away.

**The key idea ★★ — an old kind of painting given a new job:**

| **Landscape painting** (tradition) | **Reportage function** (new) |
|---|---|
| "Traditionally served the **sociocultural elite class**" | "**Specific location and time**" |
| "**A philosophical subject**" | "**Ink washes and red pigment** — to mimic smoke effects; to depict his impressions of the Japanese attacks on Chongqing as it burned" |

**In plain words:** Chinese landscape painting was normally a quiet, timeless subject for educated gentlemen. Huang uses the same brush and ink to do a news reporter's job: this city, this day, this bombing. The soft ink wash that usually paints mist now paints smoke.

One slide puts film stills of the real smoke over the city beside the painting: camera record and ink record of the same kind of scene. A map shows the bombing zones of 1939–1941.

### 2b. Guilin — Cai Dizhi, *Fleeing Guilin by the North Station* (1944), woodcut ★

- **Cai Dizhi 蔡廸支:** "a student at **Guangzhou Municipal Art School**; moved to Guilin in **1943**."
- Context: "**1944: Refugees fleeing Guilin as Japanese incursions spread to the region that year.**"
- **What you see:** a crowd of refugees packed edge to edge: a mother with a baby, an old bearded man, people with bundles. Behind them railway wagons are covered in people: sitting on the roofs under umbrellas, climbing the sides, hauling bundles up by rope.
- **How it works:** "**Shading and contrasts of black and white → desperation and terror.**"
- **Compared with:** **Jiang Feng, *Kill the Resisters*** (1931) from Week 6.

| | Jiang Feng, 1931 | Cai Dizhi, 1944 |
|---|---|---|
| Cutting | Rough, jagged | Fine, detailed |
| Figures | Mask-like, flat | Individual faces, shaded |
| Space | Almost none | Deep |

**In plain words:** the woodcut of the 1930s (Week 6) is still the medium for showing ordinary people's suffering, but by 1944 it has become more detailed and realistic.

### 2c. Chongqing — Ding Cong, *Images of Today* 現象圖 (1945) ★★

- **Ding Cong:** "**a cartoonist**".
- Ink and gouache on paper, **28.6 × 149.3 cm**: a long horizontal handscroll. (Spencer Museum of Art, University of Kansas.)
- The banner in the painting reads 甲申歲暮: "**The ending days of lunar year of 1944 → 1945**".
- "**Figures: intellectual, merchant, painter, soldier, government official, profiteer, journalist, etc.**"
- "**Social and political satire: corruption, bureaucratism, wasteful hoarding, poverty.**" ← learn these four targets

**In plain words:** it is a parade of about thirty cartoon characters showing what was wrong in the Nationalist wartime capital: the powerful are fat and corrupt, the useful people are starving or silenced.

**Reading the scroll (what the slides zoom in on).** The slides show these details as pictures only, without labels. Matching each one to a "type" on her list is my reading.

| What you see | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| A man with newspapers and a pen whose **eyes are covered by a hand from outside the picture** and whose **mouth is sealed with paper** | The **journalist**: not allowed to see or speak |
| Hunched poor people with a carrying pole and a crying child, while a **shiny black car** with a made-up woman drives past | **Poverty** next to luxury |
| Two **soldiers**: thin, one wrapped in a blanket, one **barefoot** | The men doing the fighting are not looked after |
| Men in uniform bent over ledgers beside a **donation box** (獻金箱) and a bulging **sack with rats** eating the spilled grain | **Government officials**: **bureaucratism**, **corruption**, **wasteful hoarding** |
| A **very fat man** in a fur hat with many badges, a woman in a fur coat on his arm, holding up his palm to a thin man with a sheet of paper | The well-fed and powerful turning away the needy |
| A **wounded soldier** with a sling, an eye patch and a crutch | Left to fend for himself |
| A man with a huge **magnifying glass and a ruler** checking a thin writer's pages | Writing being inspected and measured |
| A **blindfolded painter** holding up a clumsy tiger scroll. The tag reads "Tiger's Roar, 50,000 yuan", and five red strips show it is **already reserved** by a minister, a manager, a chairman, a commissioner and a "master" | The **painter**: bad art sells at a high price to the powerful. The art market is corrupt too |
| Two men in long gowns making a quiet deal; a fat man slumped over a table of dishes | The **merchant** and the **profiteer** |
| A thin man in glasses and a scholar's gown with a basket of vegetables, ragged children, a wife with a crying baby, **a child chewing paper**, books on the floor | The **intellectual**: educated and starving |

### 2d. Dunhuang — Zhang Daqian (slides 27–34) ★★

**Dunhuang**
- "**Northern Gansu province**"; "a desert area on the border of Central Asia and China".
- "**2nd century BCE to the 15th century CE: an important trading town on the Silk Routes**."
- "**5–14th century: a Buddhist center for worship**."
- Pictures: "**Buddhist cave temples built into cliff**"; "**murals**".
- "**Rediscovered in the 20th century.**" "Archaeological, anthropological, architecture investigations."
- "**China's native artistic cultures.**"

**Zhang Daqian 張大千 (1899–1983)**
- "**A renowned painter from Sichuan.**"
- "**Stayed in Dunhuang for 2.5 years during wartime (1941–1943).**"
- "**Copied 276 murals.**"
- Photo: "Zhang and his assistants copy murals at Yulin Caves, May 1943".

***Portrait of Guanyin from Dunhuang Fresco* (1943)**
- **What you see:** a full-length standing Guanyin in flat, bright colours with firm outlines: red skirt, green robe, blue scarves. A round halo, an ornate crown, layers of bead necklaces. One hand holds a willow branch, the other a small vase. Bare feet on a lotus.
- **The features it shares with the original cave paintings:**
  - "**Ornate halo**"
  - "**Jewelries (crown, multi-layered necklaces, armlets)**"
  - "**Lotus seat or throne**"
- He "**imitated the cave murals' imageries, styles and techniques**".
- The originals on the slide are worn and darkened; his copy is fresh and bright.
- A three-way comparison: Dunhuang fresco | **Tang Yin (1470–1523), *Autumn Wind Fan*** | Zhang's Guanyin. Tang Yin's lady is thin ink line with almost no colour. The Dunhuang style, heavy with colour and ornament, looks completely different.
- So Dunhuang art was "**considered as the art of the long lost, rediscovered historical epochs**".
- "Sold for HKD 48,045,500 at Sotheby's, 2019."

**In plain words:** with the coast occupied, Zhang went to the far north-west and copied ancient Buddhist wall paintings. This showed that China had a great, colourful, **native** art much older than the familiar ink tradition. In wartime, that is a source of national pride. In her conclusion this is the method "**deployment of the Chinese cultural heritage**".

## Part 3 — Zone 2: the Japanese-controlled areas (slides 35–42) ★★

"e.g. **Beijing and Shanghai**"

### Jiang Zhaohe 蔣兆和 (1904–1986), *Refugees* (1943), ink and colour on paper

**The artist**
- "**1920: taught himself western oil painting.**"
- "**1928: became a teacher at National Central University.**"

**The work — facts to know**

| | |
|---|---|
| Size | "Originally **a huge scroll (2 m high and 26 m long)**" |
| Figures | "**Life-sized; more than a hundred figures**" |
| Subject | "**Portrays Chinese people's suffering under the Japanese Occupation**" |
| First showing | "**Intended for exhibition in Beijing; forced to close**" |
| Second showing | "**Later for exhibition in Shanghai; 'borrowed' by the Japanese, half of it lost**" |

The slides show the full scroll in an old black-and-white record, then the colour fragments that survive, with blank space where the rest used to be.

**Her four points about how it works** (each one is written over a detail of the painting):

| Slide wording | The detail it sits on | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| "**Drawn from self-hired life models**" | A loaded donkey, and a family kneeling round a dying old man | He paid real people to pose. (Same academic method as Xu Beihong in Week 5.) |
| "**Chinese ink wash + European Realism**" | Close-up of the dying man's head | The face is shaded to look solid (European). The clothes are a few free ink strokes (Chinese). |
| "**The poor, vulnerable, and the disadvantaged**" | Bodies on the ground, a huddle of frightened women and children, a mother carrying a limp little girl | His subject is the weakest victims, not soldiers or heroes. |
| "**Soliciting empathy from the viewer**" | Zoom on that mother's face | Life-size figures with real faces make you feel for them. |

**In plain words:** painting this inside occupied Beijing was dangerous. There are no Japanese soldiers in the picture, only victims. Even so the show was shut down, and half the scroll disappeared.

## Part 4 — Zone 3: the international sphere (slides 43–49) ★★

"**To solicit political or financial supports.**"

### Zhang Shuqi 張書旗 (1901–1957), *Messengers of World Peace* (1940) → political support
- "**A *guohua* professor at the National Central University** from Nanjing."
- Ink and colour on paper. **What you see:** a great flock of **doves**, mostly white, sweeping in a long curve from the ground at lower left up into the sky at upper right, with flowering branches around them.
- "**A diplomatic gift**":
  - "**to arouse American sympathy to the exiled Chinese nationalist regime and its people**"
  - "**Inspired the US support for the Chinese struggle against Japan**"
- Compared with an old painting labelled "**Bird and flower painting as a traditional subject matter**".

**In plain words:** birds and flowers are a traditional Chinese subject. Zhang fills the picture with doves, which stand for peace, and sends it to America as a gift. A gentle traditional painting becomes a tool of diplomacy.

### Xu Beihong 徐悲鴻 (1895–1953), *Four Horses* (1940) → financial support
- "**Since 1939: undertook a long fundraising trip to Singapore and India.**"
- "**Horse**":
  - "**a popular subject in the Southeast Asian diaspora**"
  - "**Symbolism: noble and heroic spirit of his nation**"
- Ink and colour on paper. **What you see:** four horses in bold black brushwork: one seen from behind, two standing together, one dark horse grazing. Accurate anatomy and foreshortening.

**In plain words:** Xu sold paintings abroad to raise money for the war. Overseas Chinese loved his horses, and the horse stood for China's brave spirit. (Week 5 already listed "Horse × nationalism" as one of his three themes.)

**Keep the two apart:** Zhang Shuqi → **doves** → **USA** → **political** support. Xu Beihong → **horses** → **Singapore and India** → **money**.
