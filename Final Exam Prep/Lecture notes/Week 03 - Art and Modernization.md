# Week 3 — Late Qing and Republican Era: Art and Modernization

## The lecture in one minute

- "Modernization" usually means factories and cities. This lecture asks: **what does modernization look like for art?**
- Her answer: art became modern when it got **institutions** — **museums** to keep and show it, **exhibitions** open to the public, and **schools** to teach it.
- Two thinkers who had lived abroad pushed this:
  - **Kang Youwei** saw Europe's ruins and museums and said China must **preserve its relics and build museums**. He also argued that Chinese painters should return to **realism**.
  - **Cai Yuanpei** said **aesthetic education** — teaching people to love beauty — could do the job religion used to do and help China become a modern country.
- The results: art schools in the 1920s, the **Palace Museum (1925)**, and the **First National Art Exhibition (1929)**.

**The whole lecture in three words (conclusion slide): preservation, display, education.**

## Words you need

| Word | Slide wording | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| **Modernization** | "The transformation from a traditional, rural, and agrarian society to a secular, urban, and industrial one. (In sociology)" | A country changes from villages, farming and tradition to cities, factories and non-religious public life |
| **Institution** | "Established to organize a particular area of life; rules, authority, routines, and recognized roles" | An organised body like a museum, school or university |
| **Institutionalization** | "The process by which something becomes established, organized, and embedded within a formal institution or system" | Something loose and private becomes organised and official |
| **Cultural relics** | — | Old objects and buildings that carry a country's history |
| **Realism** | "Representation of people, places, and things in a believable and recognizable way" | Painting things so they look like the real thing |
| **Literati painting** | — | Ink painting by educated scholar-gentlemen; values brush line and personal expression more than likeness |
| **Aesthetic education (*meiyu*)** | Cai Yuanpei's term | Educating people's sense of beauty |
| ***Jinshi*** | "The highest and final degree in the imperial exam" | The top pass in the old civil-service exam |

## Part 2 — Kang Youwei (1858–1927) (slides 6–9)

### Who he was
- "A Chinese **scholar, political reformer, and intellectual** of the late Qing dynasty and early Republican periods."
- A native of **Guangdong**, born to an **official family**.
- Learned **Confucianism** from childhood, **later learned Western theories and political views**.
- Obtained the ***jinshi***, the highest degree in the imperial exam, **at the age of 37**.
- He belonged to "the generation experiencing the sociopolitical turmoils of Qing China".

### The turmoil his generation lived through

| Event | Slide facts |
|---|---|
| **Taiping Rebellion (1851–1864)** | A **14-year civil war** between the **Manchu-led Qing dynasty** and the **Hakka-led Taiping Heavenly Kingdom**. It eventually failed. It **ravaged 17 provinces** and caused **more than 20 million deaths** |
| **First Sino-Japanese War (1894)** | **Qing China lost.** The government signed the **Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895)**, giving **Taiwan, Penghu and the Liaodong Peninsula** to Japan. 1895 was also the year Kang got his top degree |

### Reform, exile and travel ★

| Year | What happened |
|---|---|
| **1898** | Kang was **a leading advocate of the Hundred Days' Reform**: "an ambitious attempt to modernize the Qing state through **institutional, educational, military, and economic** reform". **It failed.** |
| **1898–1913** | He **lived in exile abroad for 16 years** |
| **1904** | Travelled through Asia (Hong Kong, Vietnam, Malaya, Thailand, Sri Lanka, India), Europe (England, Denmark, Sweden, Germany, Belgium, Holland, France), **Italy (Herculaneum, Pompeii, Naples, Rome, Milan)** and North America |
| **1911** | The Qing dynasty collapsed |
| **1913** | **Kang returned to China, "bringing two impacts"** |

**The two impacts are the two theories below.** Learn them as a pair: **(1) cultural relics conservation, (2) realism.**

## Part 3 — Impact 1: Theory of Cultural Relics Conservation (slides 10–18)

### What Kang saw in Europe

All three slides carry the heading **"Cultural relics and art: conservation, research and display."**

| Slide | What is shown |
|---|---|
| **Rome, Italy** | The **tombs of the Caesars** (28 BCE), a round ruined mausoleum; and the **Colosseum** (70–80 AD). Ancient monuments kept standing |
| **Ancient cities in Italy** | **Herculaneum** and **Pompeii** — whole excavated ancient towns you can walk through |
| **Museums in Europe** | The **Uffizi** (Florence, Italy), the **Louvre** (Paris, France), the **British Museum** (London, Britain) |

A line on the museums slide gives his reaction: **"Seeing cultural relics in person is like experiencing relevant histories!!!"**

### The contrast with China ★

**Slide 13:** "In traditional China, valuable objects were fallen into the hands of the **social elites and political powers**."

The slides show this with pictures:
- Two Ming-dynasty paintings of **scholars privately enjoying art**: *The Eighteen Scholars* (anonymous) and *Appreciating Antiques at Bamboo Garden* (Qiu Ying). In both, a small group of educated men look at paintings and antiques in a private garden.
- **Slide 14** sorts collecting into two kinds: **imperial collection** (a Qing emperor's portrait and a palace gate) and **private collection** (collectors sitting among their bronzes).

**In plain words:** in Europe ordinary people could walk into a museum. In China, treasures were locked away with the emperor or with rich collectors. The public never saw them.

### What Kang proposed ★

After returning in **1913** he "wrote a long essay".

**He advocated two things:**
1. **Preservation of art and historical relics**
2. **Building museums**

**Four benefits he listed:**
1. "to cultivate people's **aesthetic senses** and abilities to appreciate art"
2. "to cultivate people's **temperaments** and develop their **wisdom**"
3. "to raise people's **national esteem and pride**"
4. "Only then would China **stand up against foreign plunders** of its treasures" — with the example of **the Eight-Nation Alliance, 1900**

### Two things that then happened in China

**1. Chen Hengque, *Viewing Paintings* (1918)** ★
- Ink and colour on paper, 87.7 × 46.6 cm, Palace Museum, Beijing.
- **What you see:** a crowded exhibition room. At the back, visitors look up at hanging scrolls on the wall. In front, a tight ring of people bends over a table of paintings. The crowd is **mixed**: men in Chinese caps and robes, men in Western coats and hats, and foreigners.
- **What it records** (from the painting's own inscription): an exhibition **held in Beijing in 1917 to raise funds for flood victims**. **Six or seven hundred paintings from major Beijing collections** were shown for **seven days, changed daily**, at **Central Park**.
- **Why it matters — the slide's three arrows:**
  - → **Public viewership** (paintings from private collections seen by the public)
  - → **Art participated in philanthropist activity** (art used for charity)
  - → **Cai Yuanpei's emphasis on aesthetic education and social responsibility**

**2. The Palace Museum opened in 1925** ★
- "**Public display of the formerly Qing imperial collection.**"
- The slide reuses the emperor's portrait and the palace gate from the "imperial collection" slide, now beside a photo of a **crowd of ordinary visitors**. The emperor's private treasures have become the public's.

## Part 4 — Impact 2: Theory of Realism (slides 19–26)

### What realism is ★

"**Representation of people, places, and things in a believable and recognizable way.** Well practiced in the European Art Tradition."

Its five features on the slide:
- **Form and details**
- **Mass and volume**
- **Proportion**
- **Shading and illumination**
- **Perspective**

The example is **Raphael**, "an Italian Renaissance painting master": his *Self Portrait* (1504–06, Uffizi) and *Saint Catherine of Alexandria* (1507, National Gallery, London). The saint is a rounded, solid-looking figure with light and shadow and a landscape receding behind her.

### The comparison that sets up Kang's argument

**Slide 20** puts two standing women side by side under the line "**→ Reflect on the aesthetics of traditional Chinese figure painting**":

| Left: Tang Yin, *Autumn Wind Fan* (Ming dynasty) | Right: Raphael, *Saint Catherine* (1507) |
|---|---|
| Ink on paper, almost no colour | Oil, full colour |
| Drawn with flowing **outlines** | Built up with **light and shadow** |
| Flat, pale face; little shading | Rounded, three-dimensional body |
| Large areas of blank paper | Deep landscape background |

**In plain words:** next to European realism, later Chinese figure painting looks flat. Kang saw this as a weakness.

### Kang's theory (1917) ★★

The slide's headline: **"Invention of an indigenous history of realism in China as a remedy for the main deficiency identified in literati painting (1917)."**

Unpack it:
- **"Main deficiency identified in literati painting"** — scholar-painting lacked realism.
- **"Remedy"** — the cure is realism.
- **"Indigenous history of realism"** — but Kang did not say "copy the West". He said **China once had its own realism**.
- **"Invention"** — this history was something Kang constructed to make his case.

**The three steps of the theory:**

| Step | Claim | Slide's example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Chinese painting during **Tang and Song** dynasty was **the best in the world due to their senses of realism**" | **Han Gan, *Night-Shining White*** (Tang): a lively white horse, modelled with soft shading. **Seated Portrait of Emperor Renzong of the Song**: a solid, coloured, individual-looking face |
| 2 | "but it had gradually fallen into **decline since the 13th century / Yuan dynasty**" | **Tang Yin, *Autumn Wind Fan*** (Ming): the flat, linear lady again |
| 3 | "**This idea was adopted by his student, Xu Beihong**" | **Xu Beihong, *Kang Youwei and His Family*** (1917) |

**Slide 24** places the Song emperor beside the Ming lady, with no heading: **Song = solid and lifelike; Ming = linear and pale.** That pair *is* the argument.

**Xu Beihong, *Kang Youwei and His Family* (1917)** — ink and colour on paper, 86 × 121 cm. Kang, white-haired in a pale robe, stands in a bright garden with his family around him. The **faces are shaded and lifelike, almost like photographs**. This is Kang's idea put into practice by his student.

**Slide 26** repeats the point with another pair: a realistic full-length portrait of a standing woman beside the same Ming ink lady.

## Part 5 — Cai Yuanpei (1868–1940) and aesthetic education (slides 27–30)

### Who he was ★
- **Studied philosophy, psychology and art history at the University of Leipzig, Germany**, in 1907–1911 and the mid-1910s.
- Became **Minister of Education** of the provisional Republican government in **1912**.
- Became **Chancellor of Peking University** in **1916**.
- "Advocated what he called **'aesthetic education' (*meiyu*)**."

### His big idea: "Replacing Religion with Aesthetic Education" (1917) ★★

The slide's chain of reasoning:

> The love of beauty could **eliminate greed and prejudice**, freeing minds from **selfishness and hatred**
> → a **new way of perceiving reality**
> → better **spiritual well-being**
> → a more **harmonious society**
> → **transform Chinese society into a modern country**

"**Modern country**" is underlined on the slide. That is the link to the lecture's theme: for Cai, art education is a route to **modernization**.

**In plain words:** people used to turn to religion to become better. Cai said the love of beauty can do that instead, and a nation of people with a trained sense of beauty will be a modern nation.

### His methods ★

| Method | The slide's picture |
|---|---|
| "Introduced an **art curriculum** to institutions of higher learning" | A photo of an art school in Shanghai |
| "Encouraged the practice of **drawing from life**" | An old printed manual of model trees (learning by copying) beside a photo of students **sketching outdoors** |
| "Emphasize the need to **absorb the best elements of Western art into Chinese art**" | A studio class drawing a nude model; a white plaster cast of a classical head |
| "Established **research society** on the future of Chinese art for the nation" | — |
| "Established **museums and exhibitions**" | — |

**In plain words:** the old way to learn painting was to copy examples from a book. The new way was to look at real things and draw them.

## Easy to mix up

| Do not confuse | With |
|---|---|
| **Kang Youwei**: political reformer; exile; *relics conservation* and *realism* | **Cai Yuanpei**: educator; Leipzig; Minister of Education; *aesthetic education* |
| Kang's benefit "raise **national esteem and pride**" | Cai's result "**harmony of society**" and "**modern country**" |
| **1898** Hundred Days' Reform (failed) | **1911** fall of the Qing; **1913** Kang's return |
| Tang–Song = **best, realistic** | Yuan onward = **decline** |
| *Viewing Paintings* (1918) shows a **1917** exhibition | The **Palace Museum** opened in **1925**; the **First National Art Exhibition** was in **1929** |
| **Imperial collection** (the emperor's) | **Private collection** (rich scholars') — both closed to the public |
| Kang's *realism* (a theory about Chinese painting's past) | Xu Beihong's practice of it (his student; more in Week 5) |
