## The lecture in one minute

- After the Second World War and a civil war, the Communist Party founded the **People's Republic of China (PRC) in 1949**. It set out to build a **socialist** nation: a society without classes, following **Marx's** idea that history moves from capitalism to socialism to communism.
- From then on, **all art had to serve politics**. Her phrase for this is **"politics in command"** ("art for politics' sake"), from **Mao's Yan'an Talks, 1942**. "Art for art's sake" was denounced.
- Three kinds of picture were put to work building the new nation:

| Medium | Chinese name | Key examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. New Year's picture** | ***nianhua*** | Lin Gang, *Zhao Guilan at the Heroes Reception* (1952) |
| **2. Propaganda picture/poster** | ***xuanchuanhua*** | *Sino-Soviet Friendship* (c. 1951); *Long Live Chairman Mao* (1957); *We and ore together make steel!* (1959) |
| **3. Oil painting** | — | Dong Xiwen, *Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation* (1952–53) |

- The rulebook artists had to follow was **socialist realism**, borrowed from the Soviet Union and adopted wholesale in the PRC in **1953**. It has **four principles**: realistic, proletarian subjects, typical scenes of daily life, and **idealistic**.
- Two techniques run through the lecture: **iconography** (you can tell at once who a figure is) and **sinicization** (making oil painting look Chinese).
- Dong Xiwen's painting was **repainted several times** as leaders were purged.

She said of this lecture: **"I'm sure, for the final quiz, I would definitely ask about socialist realism, because it's so important."**

## Part 1 — What "socialist nation-building" means (slides 1–15)

### Nation-building (slide 2)

Slide wording: "A coordinated political, social, and institutional project aimed at creating or reconstructing a cohesive nation-state. Involves establishing functioning institutions, governance systems, & shared national identity (not previously existed or have collapsed)."

**In plain words:** building a country's institutions, its government and the feeling of "we are one nation". You met the Nationalist version in Week 4 (the Nanjing Decade). This week is the **socialist** version.

Slide 3 asks the question the lecture answers: **"Socialist nation-building — what about a socialist one?"** The title slides show Dong Xiwen's *Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation* behind the words: it is the lecture's key picture.

### Karl Marx's "Five Modes of Social Formation" (1859) ★ (slides 4–12)

The slides build up a list, one stage at a time, each with a picture.

| Stage | Slide's label | Picture on the slide |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Primitive society** | — | Early people sharing food and animals in the open |
| 2. **Slave society** | "(ruling class)" | An engraving of a line of chained slaves driven by armed men |
| 3. **Feudalism** | "(monarchy)" | The "Feudal Pyramid of Power": King → Nobles → Knights (vassals) → Peasants. Land goes down; money, service and food go up |

| 5. **Communism** | — | (the final goal) |

**Socialism** sits on an arrow between capitalism and communism. It is the stage in which the proletariat takes power, on the way to communism.

- A black-and-white drawing titled **"Toward a free and equal society"**, with the line **"With a new world in our hearts"** along the bottom.

**The proletariat** (slide 11)
- Slide wording: **"The Proletariat: Peasants × workers; Sickle × hammer."**
- The **Soviet Union's flag** carries the crossed **sickle and hammer** (with a star).
- The Soviet poster shows a woman holding wheat (a peasant) and a man (a worker) raising the hammer and sickle together, in front of a sunburst and the red star of the Kremlin tower. "Class struggle — Revolution!"

> **In plain words:** in Marx's theory, the workers and peasants at the bottom of the pyramid rise up, overthrow the class above them, and build a society with no classes. That is the goal the new Chinese state claimed for itself.

## Part 2 — Two kinds of picture: New Year's pictures and propaganda posters (slides 16–28)

### 1. The New Year's picture (*nianhua*) ★★ (slides 16–23)

**Before 1949: the traditional New Year's picture** (slides 16–18)
- "A form of art **purchased by families to decorate their homes**" at the **beginning of the new year**.
- **Pasted** on interior spaces or on exterior doors or gates.
- **Traditional subjects: folk, secular** (not religious art for temples, and not elite art), **associated with the peasantry or rural consumers**.
- Examples on the slides:
  - **A pair of military door gods holding a sword**, pasted on the two halves of a wooden door.
  - **A cattle busy with spring plough in the new year.**
  - **Four deities riding on two Chinese unicorns** (*qilin*) **to report good news.**

**After 1949: the new-style New Year's picture in Mao China** (slide 19) ★
- **Politics in command:** artists of **all visual media were organized** to design **new-style New Year's pictures**.
- **Revolutionary themes** became the **official style**.
- **Mass-produced (millions of copies)** in the form of **magazine illustrations and posters**.
- The aim: **popularization** — to reach everyone.
- The example: a spread from ***Renmin huabao* (China Pictorial) no. 2 (1951)**, pages headed 新年畫 ("New New Year's Pictures"). Its pictures include Mao walking with Stalin, Mao talking with peasants, a defend-peace scene, a woman train driver, and Lin Gang's Zhao Guilan picture.

> **In plain words:** the Party kept a form that peasants already loved and bought every year, but filled it with new, political content. That is why it reached people so well.

**Dong Xiwen, *Liberation of Beiping*, 1949** (slide 20)
- A crowded, brightly coloured street scene: people celebrate as soldiers ride in on trucks, with two large leader portraits carried in front.
- The slide translates three banners: **"Salute to the People's Liberation Army"**, **"Welcome the Liberation Army"**, **"Carry the revolution to the end."**
- Note it is by **the same artist** as *Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation* (Part 5), but here in a crowded, flat, decorative manner, not academic oil painting.
- "Beiping" was the city's name before it became the capital and was renamed Beijing in 1949.

### 2. The propaganda picture/poster (*xuanchuanhua*) ★★ (slides 24–28)

Slide wording:
- **Learned from the Soviet** Union.
- **Images + political slogan.**
- **Production:** **production teams and publishing houses in major cities**; **millions of prints during 1954–1966**.
- **Subject:** **political movements and policy**; **social programs launched by the state**.
- **Style: realistic, bright colours, light and shade, perspective.**

**She said** (recap): it combines images and political slogans to promote the state's ideas and its political or economic campaigns; it was "a category of visual medium introduced from the Soviet Union". The first half of the lecture moved from the New Year's picture to the propaganda poster.

**Poster A: *Sino-Soviet Friendship*** (Beijing Xinhua Publishing, **c. 1951**) (slides 24–25) ★
- **Stalin and Mao shake hands** under their two flags. White doves fly at the top.
- The slide's **iconography** list:
  - **Joseph Stalin** (the Soviet Union) and **Mao Zedong** (PRC), known by **clothing, hairstyle and facial features**.
  - **The Soviet flag: crossed sickle and hammer.**
  - **The Chinese flag: the five-star red flag.**
  - **Foreground and background: agricultural products** (fruit, vegetables, cotton, grain), **industry** (factories, cranes), **white pigeons/doves** (peace).
- **Message:** the two socialist nations are close friends, and friendship brings peace, good harvests and industry.

**Poster B: *"With the great support of the Soviet Union, and our own greatest strength, we will realize the industrialization of our nation step by step!"*** (East China People's Fine Arts Publishing House, **Shanghai, December 1953**) (slides 26–27)
- A Soviet man and a Chinese man shake hands in front of a steel plant and a crane, between the two flags.
- **The Chinese man holds *The Five-Year Development Plan for the People's Republic of China*.**
- **The Russian holds *The Plan for Developing and Redesigning 141 Factories for China*.**
- **Message:** China will industrialise with Soviet help, step by step.

**Poster C: *"Study the advanced production experience of the Soviet Union, struggle for the industrialization of our country"*** (People's Fine Arts Publishing House, **Beijing, April 1953**) (slide 28)
- A Soviet expert points upward and onward. A Chinese worker in a cap stands beside him, holding a plan. Factory chimneys behind.
- The heading advertises a "Great Friendship" picture exhibition run by the Tianjin Sino-Soviet Friendship Association.
- **Message:** learn from Soviet experience to industrialise China.

> **Pattern in Part 2:** in the early 1950s China is "**leaning toward the Soviet Union**" (slide 14). The Soviet Union appears as friend, teacher and helper in nation-building, and the poster medium itself was learned from the Soviets.

## Part 3 — Socialist realism ★★ (slides 29–31; she said she will ask this)

### What the name means

Break the name in two.

- **Realism** — you already know this from Xu Beihong (Week 5): painting things so they look real, with correct shapes, proportions and space.
- **Socialist** — connected to **socialism**, the ideology of the **Soviet Union**. After the Second World War the world was split into two camps, **capitalism and socialism**. This is the socialist camp's kind of realism.

### The facts on the slide

Slide wording:
1. "**Ideological guidance** for artistic creations in **the Soviet Union and other socialist countries**, during **1934–1980s**."
2. "**Wholesale adoption in the PRC in 1953.**"

**She said:**
- **"It's not specifically a style."** It is a set of doctrines, or instructions, for people who make art.
- Some left-wing Chinese artists knew about it earlier, but it was **not enforced across China by the state until 1953**.
- It applies to every kind of art: literature, painting, photography.

### The four guiding principles ★★

Learn these four in order, in the slide's wording. A likely question lists four items and asks which one does **not** belong.

1. **Realistic (not abstract)**
2. **Proletarian subjects (easy to understand)**
3. **Typical scene of people's daily lives**
4. **Idealistic**
   - **to exemplify communist value**
   - **to educate**
   - **to instil hope, optimism and bright future**

**1. Realistic**
- The picture must look real. You can tell at once who and what is shown.
- The slide's Soviet example: ***Stalin and Voroshilov in the Kremlin*, 1938, by Aleksandr Gerasimov**. The two leaders stand by an iron railing with the Kremlin towers behind. The figures have correct proportions and a sense of volume; the background shows clearly where they stand.
- **Why realism?** So that **everyone can read the picture**, from intellectuals down to farmers and workers with little education. Art had to serve the masses.
- **The opposite is abstraction**, which was what artists in the capitalist West were pursuing. So choosing realism also marked an **ideological divide** between the two camps.

**2. Proletarian subjects**
- Paint **peasants and workers**. Do not paint the lives of the social elite or the middle class.
- Reason: a socialist country is trying to get rid of class divisions, so its art **glorifies the working class**. Ordinary people can see that art now serves them.
- The slide's Soviet examples: one oil painting of **workers straining at a furnace in a factory**, one of **peasant women walking through a field with rakes**.

**3. Typical scenes of people's daily lives**
- Show people doing the thing that **represents their social role**.
- Workers should be shown **working in a factory**. Farmers should be shown **working in the fields**.
- Not people doing something unrelated to their role.

**4. Idealistic**
- Show the **ideal**, "beyond the real". Real life has good and bad parts; this art shows only a positive message.

| It must… | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Exemplify** communist values | Be a model of ideas such as "everybody is equal, there is no class divide" |
| **Educate** the public | Works were shown in exhibitions and printed in newspapers and magazines to teach these values |
| **Instil** hope, optimism and a bright future | Make viewers feel they are heading to a bright future under communism |

### What follows from this

- Art made this way **immediately serves political and propaganda purposes**.
- For many Chinese artists in the early 1950s this was **difficult**, because art had not carried that kind of ideological job before.
- In the 1950s the figures are given **a very healthy look: strong and heroic**.

### One-line memory aid

**Real, working people, typical scene, ideal — to exemplify, educate, instil.**

## Part 4 — Three propaganda posters tested against the four principles (slides 32–39)

The slides show each poster first with only its title, then beside the list of four principles. She used each one to practise spotting the principles. Expect a question that describes a poster's features and asks which principle they show, or what message the poster carries.

### Poster 1: *Long Live Chairman Mao* 毛主席萬歲 (slides 32–34)

**Published by:** Shanghai People's Art Publishing (上海人民美術出版社), **November 1957**.

**Slide's notes:** "A **happy and healthy mother and her daughter**. The **National Parade on Tian'anmen Square in Beijing on October 1**. **Smiling towards the Chairman.**" A photo shows the ***huabiao* column in front of Tiananmen**.

**What you see** (her explanation)
- A **mother with her little daughter** on her shoulders. Both are smiling and looking up towards a top corner. The girl waves a flower.
- In that corner, faintly drawn, is a **huabiao column**. This is the decorated column that stands in front of **Tiananmen Gate** in Beijing, so it works as a symbol of the political centre.
- That tells us where they are: on **Chang'an Avenue**, the big street between Tiananmen Gate and Tiananmen Square, **where national parades take place**, on the morning of the **National Day** parade (1 October).
- **Flowers and ribbons** fly around them. **Light** shines on their faces from the same direction they are looking.
- The mother is well dressed and wears a pearl earring and a brooch.

**What it means**
- Looking towards the huabiao column means **looking towards Chairman Mao**.
- Under the Party and Mao, ordinary people feel **hopeful**.

**Against the four principles**

| Principle | How the poster shows it |
|---|---|
| Realistic | Not abstract at all; you read it at once |
| Proletarian subjects | Ordinary people, a mother and child |
| Typical daily life | **The weakest fit**: a parade day is "less everyday", but it is still ordinary people's life |
| **Idealistic** | **The strongest**: smiles, optimism about the nation's future, light as a symbol of hope, flowers as a sign of a hopeful, energetic future |

### Poster 2: *Soviet experts visit a rural commune* 蘇聯專家參觀農業社 (slides 35–36)

**Published by:** Shanghai huapian chubanshe (上海畫片出版社), **July 1957**.

**Slide's notes:** "**Healthy peasant. Good harvest. Sino-Soviet friendship.**"

**What you see**
- A **greenhouse** (grid structure in the top half) with **tomato** plants and a basket of tomatoes in the bottom half.
- In the centre, a **young peasant girl**, nicely dressed, kneels and **shows a ripe tomato to a Soviet expert**.
- You can tell he is Soviet from his face, his long brown coat, shiny shoes, hat, and the **camera** at his side.
- Behind them: a Chinese man, and a well-dressed woman who looks like an intellectual and is probably the **translator**. Further back, a **woman Soviet expert** surrounded by women peasants.

**How the peasants look**
- **Healthy**: round faces, not thin; a reddish skin tone; light reflecting off good skin.
- **Happy**: they are enjoying a **good harvest**.

**What it means**
- **A good harvest is the result of socialist leadership.** Redistribution of resources, nationalisation, collectivisation of land and land reform are shown as giving farmers a better life.
- Chinese farming also benefits from **the advice of Soviet experts**, arranged by the state: **Sino-Soviet friendship**.

**A rural commune** is a way of organising the peasants of an area so that they farm together.

### Poster 3: *We and ore together make steel!* 我們將同礦石一起煉成鋼 (slides 37–38)

**Published by:** Hubei People Publishing (湖北人民出版社), **January 1959**.

**Slide's notes:** "**Backyard furnace during The Great Leap Forward (1958–59). A workers' study class. Healthy, strong, heroic appearance. Pig iron as a good harvest!**" A photo shows a field full of backyard furnaces.

**First, the background: the Great Leap Forward**
- A political and economic campaign **started by Chairman Mao**, running **1958 to 1960** (the slide's caption dates the backyard furnaces to 1958–59).
- Aim: to **speed up** China's production, both **farming and industry**. More crops would feed the cities, so farming supported industrial growth.
- Slogan: **"Greater, faster, better, and more economical."**
- **Everyone** was pushed to join in by making steel with whatever they had. People in the countryside built **backyard furnaces**: low-tech, home-made. They were asked to hand over the metal they had at home.
- Steel made this way was poor. It is called **pig iron**.
- Mao wanted to move fast; **moderates in the Party, including Zhou Enlai and Liu Shaoqi, wanted a gradual change**. Mao's radical view won.
- The result was the **Great Famine**, in which a great many people died.

**What you see in the poster**
- A **young woman and a young man** together **hold up a lump of pig iron** above their heads. He also holds a long iron tool.
- The writing on their shirts shows they are **medical students from Wuhan**. University students were not sent to medical classes; they joined the steel-making effort instead.
- Smaller scenes behind them: young people at a **study class** (the slide calls it "a workers' study class"), and people working at a **backyard furnace**.
- The pair stand on the **central vertical axis**, in the **foreground**, filling about **half the poster**. They are very eye-catching.
- They look **young, healthy, optimistic and happy**: "healthy, strong, heroic".

**What it means**
- Everyone in the country, **even university students in the city**, had to join the Great Leap Forward.
- The steel was poor, but **they are still proud of it**: "**pig iron as a good harvest!**"

**Against the principles**

| Principle | How the poster shows it |
|---|---|
| Realistic | Every detail can be read clearly |
| Ordinary people | Not farmers or workers but **students** — "even students are joining the state initiative" |
| Typical daily life | Steel-making *was* everyday life during this campaign |
| **Idealistic** | They **enjoy** taking part; hopeful, firm, committed — "very idealistic… and also ideological" |

> This same poster comes back in Week 11, where it is set beside *Molten Steel & Sweat* (1981) to show how post-Mao painters stopped idealising steel work.

Slide 39 points to the source of most of these posters: **Chinese Poster Net** (chineseposters.net).

## Part 5 — 3. Oil painting: Dong Xiwen, *Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation* (1952–53) ★★ (slides 40–47)

Oil painters were also "remodelled" by state institutions to serve the new nation. This is the key example.

### Basic facts (slides 40–41)

| | |
|---|---|
| Artist | **Dong Xiwen**, a famous oil painter (also the painter of *Liberation of Beiping*, 1949) |
| Date | **1952–53** (original version) |
| Medium | **Oil on canvas**, very large |
| Subject | **1 October 1949**: Chairman Mao announces the founding of the PRC and its new government |
| Place | The upper level of **Tiananmen Gate**, a structure from the imperial dynasties that became **a new icon of the PRC** after 1949. In front is **Chang'an Avenue**; the space beyond later became Tiananmen Square |
| Why made | "**For museum display in 1952–53.** To **celebrate the 4th anniversary of the PRC**. **Instil a sense of nationalism in the viewers.**" |
| How it spread | "**Icon of the new Chinese nation.** **Reproduced as a poster and published in newspapers and magazines.**" |

### Who is in it (slide 43)

- **The centre: Chairman Mao Zedong**, "announcing the establishment of the PRC and its new government on October 1, 1949". You know him from his hairstyle and clothing (this is **iconography**).

| Row | People (slide's list, left to right) |
|---|---|
| **1st row** | **General Zhu De** (Head of the PLA), **Liu Shaoqi**, **Song Qingling**, Li Jisheng, Zhang Lan, **Gao Gang** |
| **2nd row** | **Premier Zhou Enlai**, Dong Biwu |
| **3rd row** | Lin Boqu |

**She said:**
- **Zhu De** — general, head of the People's Liberation Army.
- **Liu Shaoqi** — a top Party leader, who in the early 1960s took over from Mao for a few years.
- **Song Qingling** — wife of Sun Yat-sen, the founding figure of modern China.
- **Gao Gang** — the man wearing **round glasses**.
- **Zhou Enlai** — the Premier, second or third in power after Mao.

### "How real is it?" — the comparison with the newsreel ★

She showed film of the real ceremony and asked what was the same and what was different.

| Same as the real event | Changed by the painter |
|---|---|
| A specific date and place | In the film, leaders stand **close around Mao** and it takes a while to find him. In the painting **Mao stands apart**, with a clear gap around him |
| The huge, excited crowd | Leaders are arranged in **neat rows**, all looking respectfully at Mao |
| Red lanterns really did hang on the gate | **Song Qingling** is clearly placed in the front row |
| | **Clear blue sky** fills about **one third** of the picture |

**What the changes do**
- You see the **hierarchy** at once: who the leader is.
- The blue sky stands for **hope and a bright future**.

**The lesson she drew:** even when a painter uses realism, **many details can be manipulated** — where figures stand, their expressions, the sky — to put a message into the work. "That's the power of visual art."

### Sinicization ★★ ("this is a big word") (slide 42)

**The idea**
- Oil painting was seen as a medium from **Europe and the United States**.
- To build the idea of a new nation, even this Western medium had to be **turned into something more Chinese**.
- That process is **sinicization**.

The slide gives **two lists**. Keep them apart.

| **"Sinicization of oil painting"** (Chinese motifs) | **"Grandeur"** (a sense of scale and occasion) |
|---|---|
| **Red lanterns** (four across the top) | **Iron railing** |
| **Ornate woodwork** (the red columns and carved beams) | **Broad boulevard** |

### The versions: a painting that records Party history ★★ (slides 44–47)

The painting was **revised several times as politics changed**. The slides compare the **original version (1952–53)**, the **3rd version (1967)** and an **"Xth version, 197X"**.

| Version | What changed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Original, 1952–53** | — | — |
| A revision | **Gao Gang removed** | Gao Gang was **purged; removed in 1954–55** |
| **3rd version, 1967** | **Liu Shaoqi removed** | Liu Shaoqi was **purged; removed in 1967**, at the start of the Cultural Revolution |
| **"Xth version, 197X"** (1970s) | **Liu Shaoqi put back**. Gao Gang still missing | Liu's position in the Party had been restored |

Slide 46 is headed **"Politically Purged"**, with photos of **Gao Gang** ("Removed in 1954–1955") and **Liu Shaoqi** ("Removed in 1967").

**Why remove them?** A purged person had been removed from the Party "institutionally and politically". So he also had to be **erased visually — erased from the memory of the nation**, even though he really was there in 1949.

**Why Liu Shaoqi in particular?**
- Mao believed in a **radical, revolutionary** way of running the country (for example, the Great Leap Forward).
- Liu believed in a **slower, moderate** way.
- After the Great Leap Forward ended in famine, the Party judged it a mistake. **Mao stepped down for a few years** and **Liu rose** in the early 1960s.
- When Mao regained his position in the mid-1960s, Liu, now his political enemy, had to go.

**How do you delete someone from a painting?** (before Photoshop)
- **Oil paint is thick.** It is applied in layers, so you can paint over what was there.
- **Chinese ink painting is thin and translucent.** It is water-based, and what you painted before is hard to change or cover.
- To remove Liu, the painter **extended Song Qingling's shoulder**, turned Liu's body into the body of a man in the second row, and covered part of Liu's head with **blue paint**.

**Two other changes in the 1967 version**

| Change | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Two more microphones added** in front of Mao (two in the original, four in 1967) | Pulls the viewer's eye from the left side (where figures were altered) **to the centre**. Everyone seems to look at Mao more attentively |
| **Some clouds removed**, more blue sky | The sky forms a **halo-like** shape around Mao's head, like the circle of light around holy figures in European religious painting. It heightens his **political sanctity** |

Students in class also noticed the colour had become redder and darker. In the 197X version the colours are lighter and brighter again.

**Why this painting matters — three reasons she gave**
1. It became an **icon of the new nation** through mass reproduction.
2. It shows how an artist could **nationalise / sinicize oil painting**.
3. Through its revisions it **"embodies the political history" of the Communist Party** in the 1950s and 60s.

## Easy to mix up

| Do not confuse | With |
|---|---|
| ***Nianhua*** (New Year's picture): folk, secular, bought by rural families to decorate homes at the new year; given revolutionary themes after 1949 | ***Xuanchuanhua*** (propaganda poster): learned from the Soviet Union, image + political slogan, millions printed 1954–1966 |
| **Realism** (Week 3, Week 5): making things look real | **Socialist realism**: looks real **and** must be about working people, typical, and idealised, to serve the state |
| **Socialism** (the proletariat in power; the stage after capitalism) | **Communism** (stage 5, the final goal in Marx's list) |
| **Feudalism** = monarchy; **slave society** = ruling class | **Capitalism** = entrepreneurship |
| **Politics in command** = art for politics' sake (Mao, Yan'an, 1942) | **Art for art's sake** (Lin Fengmian, Storm Society) — denounced and terminated |
| Socialist realism used in the Soviet Union **1934–1980s** | **Wholesale adoption in the PRC in 1953** |
| **Zhongnanhai** (Party headquarters; Lin Gang's *Heroes Reception*) | **Tiananmen Gate** (Dong Xiwen's *Inaugural Ceremony*; the huabiao column in *Long Live Chairman Mao*) |
| Lin Gang, *Zhao Guilan at the Heroes Reception*, **1952, ink and colour on silk, New Year's picture** | Dong Xiwen, *Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation*, **1952–53, oil on canvas** |
| Dong Xiwen, ***Liberation of Beiping*** (1949): crowded street celebration with banners | Dong Xiwen, ***Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation*** (1952–53): Mao on Tiananmen Gate |
| Poster B: **Chinese** man holds the **Five-Year Development Plan** | Poster B: **Russian** holds the plan for **141 Factories** for China |
| *Sino-Soviet Friendship* (c. 1951): **Stalin and Mao** shake hands | *Stalin and Voroshilov in the Kremlin* (1938, Gerasimov): Soviet socialist realism example |
| **Sinicization** motifs: lanterns, ornate woodwork, marble parapet, porcelain flowerpots, silk carpet | **Grandeur**: iron railing, broad boulevard, flag-waving citizens, yellow-roofed gates |
| **Propaganda poster**: printed image + slogan, from the Soviet Union | **Dazibao** (Week 10): handwritten big characters attacking a named person |
| **Gao Gang**: purged 1954–55, removed first, never put back | **Liu Shaoqi**: purged 1967, removed in the 3rd version, put back in the 1970s |
| **Sinicization** (making oil painting look Chinese) | **Guohua** (Week 4): ink painting named China's national painting |
| The **Great Leap Forward** (1958–60) | The **Cultural Revolution** (1966–76) |
| "We and ore together make steel!" — students, proud, idealised | *Molten Steel & Sweat* (Week 11) — a tired worker, not idealised |
