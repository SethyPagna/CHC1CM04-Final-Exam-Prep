## The lecture in one minute

- In 1949 the Communist Party founded the People's Republic of China (PRC). From then on, **all art had to serve politics**. Her phrase for this is **"politics in command"**.
- Every kind of picture was put to work building the new nation: **New Year pictures, propaganda posters, oil paintings**.
- The rulebook artists had to follow was **socialist realism**, borrowed from the Soviet Union. It has **four principles**: realistic, about ordinary working people, showing typical scenes, and **idealised**.
- Posters of the 1950s therefore show people who are **healthy, strong, happy and hopeful**, whatever real life was like.
- The most famous oil painting of the period, **Dong Xiwen's *Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation***, was made to look "Chinese" (**sinicization**) and was **repainted several times** as leaders fell out of favour.

She said of this lecture: **"I'm sure, for the final quiz, I would definitely ask about socialist realism, because it's so important."**

## Words you need

| Word | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| **Politics in command** | Art is not free. Its job is to serve the Party's political goals. |
| **Proletariat / proletarian** | Ordinary working people: **workers, peasants (farmers)** and soldiers. The opposite of the wealthy, educated elite (the "bourgeois"). |
| **Propaganda poster** | A printed poster with **a picture plus a political slogan**, made in huge numbers to spread the state's message. |
| **Socialist realism** | A set of rules from the Soviet Union telling artists what art should show and how. Not just a look — an **ideology**. |
| **Idealistic** | Showing life as it *should* be under socialism — happy, healthy, hopeful — rather than as it really was. |
| **Great Leap Forward** | Mao's campaign of 1958–60 to make farming and industry grow very fast. |
| **Backyard furnace** | A small home-made furnace. Ordinary people were told to make steel in them during the Great Leap Forward. |
| **Pig iron** | The poor-quality metal those furnaces produced. |
| **Sinicization** | Making something that came from abroad look and feel **Chinese**. ("Sino-" means Chinese.) |
| **Purge** | Removing a person from the Party and from power because they are no longer wanted. |
| **Iconography** | The set of visual clues — face, hair, clothes, objects — that tell you who a figure is. |
| **Halo** | The glowing circle around the head of a holy figure in religious paintings. |

## Part 1 — What we know of the first half

This is only what she said when recapping. Check it against the real slides when you have them.

**Politics in command**
- The idea that **art has to serve a political purpose**.
- Mao Zedong first set it out fully in **1942**, in a series of meetings at **Yan'an**. Yan'an was the Communist Party's headquarters ("stronghold") during the war.
- At first it was followed mainly by **left-wing artists**.
- After the Communists founded the **PRC in 1949**, with the capital in **Beijing**, and pushed the Nationalist Party out of mainland China, art and culture had to serve politics **completely**.

**The kinds of pictures covered**
- The first half moved from the **New Year poster (New Year picture)** to the **propaganda poster**.
- A **propaganda poster** combines **images and political slogans** to promote the state's ideas and its political or economic campaigns.
- It was **a category of visual medium introduced from the Soviet Union**.

**The social background she referred back to**
- The new state wanted to turn a society that favoured the elite into a socialist one where **class divisions and hierarchy are erased**.
- **Land reform** (taking land from landlords and collectivising it) was part of this.

## Part 2 — Socialist realism ★★ (she said she will ask this)

### What the name means

Break the name in two.

- **Realism** — you already know this from Xu Beihong (Week 5): painting things so they look real, with correct shapes, proportions and space.
- **Socialist** — connected to **socialism**, the ideology of the **Soviet Union**. After the Second World War the world was split into two camps, **capitalism and socialism**. This is the socialist camp's kind of realism.

### The four facts about where it came from

1. It is **"an ideological guidance. It's not specifically a style."** It is a set of doctrines, or instructions, for people who make art.
2. It comes from the **Soviet Union**, where it was in force from **1934 until the 1980s**.
3. Some left-wing Chinese artists knew about it earlier, but it was **not enforced across China by the state until 1953**.
4. It applies to every kind of art: literature, painting, photography.

### The four guiding principles ★★

Learn these four in order. A likely question lists four items and asks which one does **not** belong.

**1. Realistic**
- The picture must look real. You can tell at once who and what is shown.
- Her Soviet example: a 1938 painting of two political leaders, including **Stalin**, at the **Kremlin** in Moscow. The figures have correct proportions and a sense of volume; the background shows clearly where they stand.
- **Why realism?** So that **everyone can read the picture**, from intellectuals down to farmers and workers with little education. Art had to serve the masses.
- **The opposite is abstraction**, which was what artists in the capitalist West were pursuing. So choosing realism also marked an **ideological divide** between the two camps.

**2. Proletarian subjects**
- Paint **peasants and workers**. Do not paint the lives of the social elite or the middle class.
- Reason: a socialist country is trying to get rid of class divisions, so its art **glorifies the working class**. Ordinary people can see that art now serves them.
- Her Soviet examples: one oil painting of workers hard at work in a factory, one of peasants working in a field.

**3. Typical scenes of people's daily life**
- Show people doing the thing that **represents their social role**.
- Workers should be shown **working in a factory**. Farmers should be shown **working in the fields**.
- Not people doing something unrelated to their role.

**4. Idealistic**
- Show the **ideal**, "beyond the real". Real life has good and bad parts; this art shows only a positive message.
- She listed three things the art must do. These three reappear on the conclusion slide:

| It must… | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Exemplify** communist values | Be a model of ideas such as "everybody is equal, there is no class divide" |
| **Educate** the public | Works were shown in exhibitions and printed in newspapers and magazines to teach these values |
| **Instil** hope and optimism | Make viewers feel they are heading to a bright future under communism |

### What follows from this

- Art made this way **immediately serves political and propaganda purposes**.
- For many Chinese artists in the early 1950s this was **difficult**, because art had not carried that kind of ideological job before.
- In the 1950s the figures are given **a very healthy look: strong and heroic**.

### One-line memory aid

**Real, working people, typical scene, ideal — to exemplify, educate, instil.**

## Part 3 — Three propaganda posters

She used each poster to practise spotting the four principles. Expect a question that describes a poster's features and asks which principle they show, or what message the poster carries.

### Poster 1: *Long Live Chairman Mao* (1957)

**What you see**
- A **mother holding her little daughter**. Both are smiling and looking up towards a top corner.
- In that corner, faintly drawn, is a **Huabiao column**. This is the decorated column that stands in front of **Tiananmen Gate** in Beijing, so it works as a symbol of the political centre.
- That tells us where they are: on **Chang'an Avenue**, the big street between Tiananmen Gate and Tiananmen Square, **where national parades take place**. It is the morning of the **National Day** parade (1 October).
- **Flowers and ribbons** fly around them. **Light** shines on their faces from the same direction they are looking.
- The mother is well dressed and wears a pearl necklace.

**What it means**
- Looking towards the Huabiao column means **looking towards Chairman Mao**.
- Under the Party and Mao, ordinary people feel **hopeful**.

**Against the four principles**

| Principle | How the poster shows it |
|---|---|
| Realistic | Not abstract at all; you read it at once |
| Proletarian subjects | Ordinary people, a mother and child |
| Typical daily life | **The weakest fit**: a parade day is "less everyday", but it is still ordinary people's life |
| **Idealistic** | **The strongest**: smiles, optimism about the nation's future, light as a symbol of hope, flowers as a sign of a hopeful, energetic future |

### Poster 2: *Soviet experts visit a rural commune*

**What you see**
- A **greenhouse** (grid structure in the top half) with a field of **tomatoes** in the bottom half.
- In the centre, a **young peasant girl**, nicely dressed, kneels and **shows a ripe tomato to a Soviet expert**.
- You can tell he is Soviet from his face, his long brown coat, shiny shoes, hat, and the **camera** at his side.
- Behind them: a Chinese man, and a well-dressed woman who looks like an intellectual and is probably the **translator**. Further back, a **woman Soviet expert** surrounded by women peasants.

**How the peasants look**
- **Healthy**: round faces, not thin; a reddish skin tone; light reflecting off good skin.
- **Happy**: they are enjoying a **good harvest**.

**What it means**
- **A good harvest is the result of socialist leadership.** Redistribution of resources, nationalisation, collectivisation of land and land reform are shown as giving farmers a better life.
- Chinese farming also benefits from **the advice of Soviet experts**, arranged by the state.

**A rural commune** is a way of organising the peasants of an area so that they farm together.

### Poster 3: *We and ore together make steel!* (Hubei People's Publishing)

**First, the background: the Great Leap Forward**
- A political and economic campaign **started by Chairman Mao**, running **1958 to 1960**.
- Aim: to **speed up** China's production, both **farming and industry**. More crops would feed the cities, so farming supported industrial growth.
- Slogan: **"Greater, faster, better, and more economical."**
- **Everyone** was pushed to join in by making steel with whatever they had. People in the countryside built **backyard furnaces**: low-tech, home-made. They were asked to hand over the metal they had at home.
- Steel made this way was poor. It is called **pig iron**.
- Mao wanted to move fast; **moderates in the Party, including Zhou Enlai and Liu Shaoqi, wanted a gradual change**. Mao's radical view won.
- The result was the **Great Famine**, in which a great many people died.

**What you see in the poster**
- A **young woman and a young man** together **hold up a lump of pig iron** above their heads.
- The writing on their shirts shows they are **medical students from Wuhan**. University students were not sent to medical classes; they joined the steel-making effort instead.
- Smaller scenes behind them: young people listening to an ideological talk, and people standing round a **backyard furnace**.
- The pair stand on the **central vertical axis**, in the **foreground**, filling about **half the poster**. They are very eye-catching.
- They look **young, healthy, optimistic and happy**.

**What it means**
- Everyone in the country, **even university students in the city**, had to join the Great Leap Forward.
- The steel was poor, but **they are still proud of it**.

**Against the principles**

| Principle | How the poster shows it |
|---|---|
| Realistic | Every detail can be read clearly |
| Ordinary people | Not farmers or workers but **students** — "even students are joining the state initiative" |
| Typical daily life | Steel-making *was* everyday life during this campaign |
| **Idealistic** | They **enjoy** taking part; hopeful, firm, committed — "very idealistic… and also ideological" |

> This same poster comes back in Week 11, where it is set beside *Molten Steel & Sweat* (1981) to show how post-Mao painters stopped idealising steel work.

## Part 4 — Dong Xiwen, *Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation* (1952–53) ★★

Oil painters were also "remodelled" by state institutions to serve the new nation. This is the key example.

### Basic facts

| | |
|---|---|
| Artist | **Dong Xiwen**, a famous oil painter |
| Date | **1952–53** |
| Medium | **Oil painting**, very large |
| Subject | **1 October 1949**: Chairman Mao announces the founding of the PRC and its new government |
| Place | The upper level of **Tiananmen Gate**, a structure from the imperial dynasties that became **a new icon of the PRC** after 1949. In front is **Chang'an Avenue**; the space beyond later became Tiananmen Square |
| Why made | The state asked artists for iconic works to celebrate the founding of the nation |
| How it spread | Shown in the museum and **reproduced as posters and in newspapers and magazines** → it became **an icon of the new Chinese nation** |

### Who is in it

- **Chairman Mao** stands at the prominent centre. You know him from his hairstyle and clothing (this is **iconography**).
- **Front row, from the left:**
  - **Zhu De** — general, head of the People's Liberation Army.
  - **Liu Shaoqi** — a top Party leader, who in the early 1960s took over from Mao for a few years.
  - **Song Qingling** — wife of Sun Yat-sen, the founding figure of modern China.
  - Others, including **Gao Gang**, the man wearing glasses.
- **Second row, far left: Zhou Enlai**, the Premier, second or third in power after Mao.

### "How real is it?" — the comparison with the newsreel ★

She showed film of the real ceremony and asked what was the same and what was different.

| Same as the real event | Changed by the painter |
|---|---|
| A specific date and place | In the film, leaders stand **close around Mao** and it takes a while to find him. In the painting **Mao stands apart**, with a clear gap around him |
| The huge, excited crowd | Leaders are arranged in **neat rows**, all looking respectfully at Mao |
| Red lanterns really did hang on the gate | **Song Qingling** is clearly placed in the front row |
| | **Clear blue sky** fills about **one third** of the picture |

**What the changes do**
- You see the **hierarchy** at once: who the leader is.
- The blue sky stands for **hope and a bright future**.

**The lesson she drew:** even when a painter uses realism, **many details can be manipulated** — where figures stand, their expressions, the sky — to put a message into the work. "That's the power of visual art."

### Sinicization ★★ ("this is a big word")

**The idea**
- Oil painting was seen as a medium from **Europe and the United States**.
- To build the idea of a new nation, even this Western medium had to be **turned into something more Chinese**.
- That process is **sinicization**.

**How Dong Xiwen did it: Chinese things, painted prominently**

| Motif | Note |
|---|---|
| **Red lanterns** | Four of them across the top |
| **Red columns** | Ornate woodwork |
| **Marble parapet** | Carved in a Chinese form (the low stone wall at the edge of the platform) |
| **Porcelain flower pots** | Porcelain carries strong Chinese cultural symbolism |
| **Silk carpet** | With traditional Chinese **cloud motifs** |
| **Yellow roof** | On the gate buildings |

**He also created grandeur:** the space stretches from the platform, past the marble parapet and an iron railing, to the very broad avenue with flags on both sides.

**Result:** the painting "looks Chinese enough" to serve nation-building.

### The versions: a painting that records Party history ★★

The painting was **revised several times as politics changed**.

| Version | What changed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Original, 1952–53** | — | — |
| First revision | **Gao Gang removed** | Gao Gang was **purged in 1954–55** |
| **Third version, 1967** | **Liu Shaoqi removed** | Liu Shaoqi was **purged in 1967**, at the start of the Cultural Revolution |
| A later version (1970s) | **Liu Shaoqi put back**. Gao Gang still missing | Liu's position in the Party had been restored |

**Why remove them?** A purged person had been removed from the Party "institutionally and politically". So he also had to be **erased visually — erased from the memory of the nation**, even though he really was there in 1949.

**Why Liu Shaoqi in particular?**
- Mao believed in a **radical, revolutionary** way of running the country (for example, the Great Leap Forward).
- Liu believed in a **slower, moderate** way.
- After the Great Leap Forward ended in famine, the Party judged it a mistake. **Mao stepped down for a few years** and **Liu rose** in the early 1960s.
- When Mao regained his position in the mid-1960s, Liu, now his political enemy, had to go.

**How do you delete someone from a painting?** (before Photoshop)
- **Oil paint is thick.** It is applied in layers, so you can paint over what was there.
- **Chinese ink painting is thin and translucent.** It is water-based, and what you painted before is hard to change or cover.
- To remove Liu, the painter **extended Song Qingling's shoulder**, turned Liu's body into the body of a man in the second row, and covered part of Liu's head with **blue paint**.

**Two other changes in the 1967 version**

| Change | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Two more microphones added** in front of Mao | Pulls the viewer's eye from the left side (where figures were altered) **to the centre**. Everyone seems to look at Mao more attentively |
| **Some clouds removed**, more blue sky | The sky forms a **halo-like** shape around Mao's head, like the circle of light around holy figures in European religious painting. It heightens his **political sanctity** |

Students in class also noticed the colour had become redder and harsher.

**Why this painting matters — three reasons she gave**
1. It became an **icon of the new nation** through mass reproduction.
2. It shows how an artist could **nationalise / sinicize oil painting**.
3. Through its revisions it **"embodies the political history" of the Communist Party** in the 1950s and 60s.

## The conclusion slide

- **Topic:** the role of art in Chinese **socialist nation-building**, **from 1949 to the early 1960s**.
- **Visual media of all kinds** — New Year pictures, propaganda posters, painting — **took part in the state's nation-building project**.
- Some used **iconography**: specific faces, clothing and motifs let you identify the figures at once (Mao; Gao Gang with his glasses).
- **Sinicization** of oil painting was another technique.
- **Socialist realism** — its function is **to exemplify, to educate, and to instil** an idealistic outlook. In the 1950s especially, figures look **healthy, strong and heroic**.
- In the **early 1960s** the political atmosphere changed, and **"sweet and lyrical" subjects were allowed**.

## Easy to mix up

| Do not confuse | With |
|---|---|
| **Realism** (Week 3, Week 5): making things look real | **Socialist realism**: looks real **and** must be about working people, typical, and idealised, to serve the state |
| **Propaganda poster**: printed image + slogan, from the Soviet Union | **Dazibao** (Week 10): handwritten big characters attacking a named person |
| **Gao Gang**: purged 1954–55, removed first, never put back | **Liu Shaoqi**: purged 1967, removed in the third version, put back in the 1970s |
| **Sinicization** (making oil painting look Chinese) | **Guohua** (Week 4): ink painting named China's national painting |
| The **Great Leap Forward** (1958–60) | The **Cultural Revolution** (1966–76) |
| "We and ore together make steel!" — students, proud, idealised | *Molten Steel & Sweat* (Week 11) — a tired worker, not idealised |
