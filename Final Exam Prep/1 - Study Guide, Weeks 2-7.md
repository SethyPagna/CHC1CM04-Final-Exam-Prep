# Week 2 — Late Qing and Early Republican: Shanghai Modern

**Big idea:** Treaty-port Shanghai became China's first modern metropolis. A new merchant class and an art market changed who paid for art (patronage), what was painted (new subjects), how it looked (bright, direct, immediate) and how people pictured themselves (self-portraits, modern women).

## 1. Historical conditions

- **Late Qing dynasty (1644–1912):** social and political upheaval, internal and external.
- **18th century:** Emperor **Qianlong** restricted maritime and foreign trade; European commerce was limited to **one port, Canton (Guangzhou)**.
- ★ **Opium War (1839–1842) → Treaty of Nanking (1842):**
  - Hong Kong Island ceded to Britain.
  - **Five ports opened: Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, Shanghai.**
  - Start of the **treaty port era (1842–1946)**.
- ★ **Shanghai = China's first modern metropolis.**
  - Geographical advantage: access to the **Grand Canal, the Huangpu River and the Pacific Ocean**.
  - Domestic and international trade; influx of capital and entrepreneurial spirit.
  - By **1876**, more than 200 foreign companies traded with Shanghai.
  - **The Bund** = the waterfront of the Huangpu River. Foreign banks (e.g. **HSBC**, building opened 1923) in **European Renaissance style**: columns, domes, arches.
  - A mercantile hub with **extraterritorial administration and concessions**.

## 2. "How modern?" — modern as a break from the past

| Traditional | Modern |
|---|---|
| Rickshaw | Tramway |
| Teahouse | Café |
| Chinese theatre | Western cinema |
| — | Dance hall, horse-racing course (after 1949: the People's Park) |

- Liang Qichao, "Advocating the Establishment of Girls' Schools" (1897).
- **A City of Sin:** courtesan culture, child prostitution.
- **A City of Opportunity:** migrants from **Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Anhui**, including artists who brought regional styles and mixed them with novel elements for a new class of collectors.
- ★ **New visual technologies: photography and lithography (printing).**

## 3. Patronage ★

- **Patron:** a person who gives financial or other support. **Patronage:** that support.

| | Traditional (Ming and Qing) | New (modernizing Shanghai) |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Personal connections | A new class of **merchant** collectors |
| How | **Artist residency**: artist lives with a family for years | **Art dealers and art shops** (fans, ink stones, paper, mounting); dealers act as agents |
| Why | — | To show taste and erudition as **social distinction** |
| Pricing | — | **Price lists** by genre, size, materials, artist's fame |
| Result | — | Professional artists gain agency; art becomes a **commodity** and a form of **urban consumption** |

## 4. The Shanghai School (Haipai) ★

**Definition:** a group of artists active in Shanghai who were **not born there but relocated there to make a living**.

### Xugu (1823–96)
- A religious (monk's) name: *xu* = empty, *gu* = valley.
- **Zen Buddhist inspiration: emptiness and negative space.**
- Ordinary subject matter: domestic animals, delicacies.
- *Cat and Butterflies*: playful white cat (erotic overtone); watercolour effect + calligraphic "bone line".
- Goldfish = auspicious.
- *Album of Various Subjects* (1895): litchi, water chestnut, peach — colourful, eye-catching, geometric repetition, **almost an abstract pattern**.

### Ren Xiong (1823–57)
- Born Xiaoshan, Zhejiang. Trained in elite arts (poetry, painting, music) **and** practical skills (archery, wrestling, riding).
- Died at about 34; considered one of the most innovative Chinese artists of the 19th century; formed the **new figure-painting style** of the Shanghai School.
- *Magu Presenting Longevity*: Daoist immortal, elixir of life, longevity. Red mineral pigment **cinnabar**. Little cultural allusion — "you see what you see" (**immediacy**).
- *The Ten Myriads*: **gold leaf** (traditionally decorative, rarely used in painting); luxurious mineral pigments.
- ★ ***Self-Portrait* (c. 1856):**
  - Context: self-portraits were **uncommon in China**; portraits were traditionally made for the **emperor or the deceased**, for commemoration (compare Castiglione's Qianlong).
  - **Monumental** figure (about five feet tall; small head, big feet).
  - **Highly naturalistic shading and light** — echoes the arrival of **photography**.
  - Emaciated, deadly serious, **staring out at the viewer**.
  - Partially uncovered body (traditional figures are fully covered); folded hands = latent energy; long fingernails = man of letters, not a labourer; sword/martial-artist reference.
  - Diagnosed with TB → sense of uncertainty. The portrait works as an **autobiography**.
  - ★ Meaning: **a powerful statement of self; projection of subjectivity**; traditional brushwork used for a documentary purpose.

### Ren Yi / Ren Bonian (1840–95)
- Son of a rice merchant who also painted portraits; father died in the Taiping Rebellion (c. 1861). From Shaoxing, Zhejiang. Student of Ren Xiong's younger brother.
- *Portraits of the Artist's Grandparent-in-law* (1885): crane = immortality, birthday; bright colour.
- *Portrait of Gao Yongzhi* (1887): pine trees = longevity, gentleman's virtue; realistic face.
- ★ *Gao Yongzhi as a Calligraphy Beggar* (1887): basket of paper, brush, ink stone; **"selling painting as if a courtesan selling smiles" → social ambivalence of an artist selling art**.
- ★ *Wu Changshi: The Shabby Official* (1888): Wu was from a gentry family, a member of the Metal and Stone School, forced to take commissions after his parents died in the Taiping Rebellion. Bitter, pitiful face = **anxiety about serving the Qing court**. Watery "boneless" colour. **Satirises the sitter.**

### Summary slide: "Social Transformation and Art" ★
- **New subject matters:** daily subjects (delicacies, domestic animals), Chinese folklore, figures and portraits.
- **New style:** colour bright and eye-catching (cinnabar); composition direct, sharp, geometrical.
- **→ novelty and visual immediacy.**

## 5. Modern women and yuefenpai ★★ (sample question)

- **Yuefenpai (calendar posters/prints), 1910s–1930s.**
- A calendar printed in **poster format**, featuring **pretty ladies, sometimes celebrities, plus the commodities** being sold.
- **Inserted in Sunday newspapers; wide circulation.**
- Purpose (her explanation of the sample answer): to promote merchandise; the pretty modern lady makes people keep the poster longer.
- Related: *Liangyou / Young Companion* magazine no. 34 (January 1929), cover with **Anna May Wong**, Chinese American Hollywood star; cartoons on materialism.

**Conclusion slide:** Modernizing Shanghai → new patronage, interests and tastes → new representation of oneself (Shanghai School painting, yuefenpai).

## Likely questions — Week 2
- Which treaty opened Shanghai, and when? *Treaty of Nanking, 1842.*
- Name the five treaty ports. *Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, Shanghai.*
- What is the Bund? *The Huangpu River waterfront with European Renaissance-style bank buildings.*
- What defines a Shanghai School artist? *Relocated to Shanghai to make a living; not born there.*
- How did new patronage differ from traditional? *Merchant collectors, dealers and shops, price lists, versus personal connection and artist residency.*
- Why is Ren Xiong's self-portrait important? *Rare genre; naturalism echoing photography; statement of self and subjectivity.*
- Who used gold leaf? *Ren Xiong, The Ten Myriads.* Who used negative space? *Xugu.*
- What does the "calligraphy beggar" portrait express? *Social ambivalence about selling art.*
- A correct feature of yuefenpai? *Featuring commodities; pretty ladies; Sunday newspapers; 1910s–30s.*

# Week 3 — Late Qing and Republican Era: Art and Modernization

**Big idea:** Modernization in art happened through **institutions**: museums, exhibitions, schools. Two overseas-returned intellectuals drove it — **Kang Youwei** (preserve relics; revive realism) and **Cai Yuanpei** (aesthetic education).

## 1. Definitions ★
- **Modernization (sociology):** transformation from a traditional, rural, agrarian society to a secular, urban, industrial one.
- **Institution:** established to organise a particular area of life; has rules, authority, routines and recognised roles.
- **Institutionalization:** the process by which something becomes established, organised and embedded within a formal institution or system.

## 2. Kang Youwei (1858–1927)

- Scholar, **political reformer**, intellectual. Native of **Guangdong**, official family; Confucian education, later Western theories.
- Obtained the highest degree (*jinshi*) at 37 — in 1895, the year of the Treaty of Shimonoseki.
- His generation lived through:
  - **Taiping Rebellion (1851–1864):** 14-year civil war, Manchu-led Qing vs Hakka-led Taiping Heavenly Kingdom; 17 provinces ravaged; over 20 million dead.
  - **First Sino-Japanese War (1894) → Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895):** Taiwan, Penghu and Liaodong Peninsula ceded to Japan.
- ★ **1898: leading advocate of the Hundred Days' Reform** (institutional, educational, military, economic) — it **failed** → **exile abroad for 16 years (1898–1913)**.
- 1904 onwards: travelled Asia, Europe (Italy: Herculaneum, Pompeii, Naples, Rome, Milan) and North America.
- 1911 Qing collapses; **1913 Kang returns**, bringing **two impacts**.

### Impact 1 — Theory of Cultural Relics Conservation ★
- What he saw: Rome (tombs of the Caesars, Colosseum), Herculaneum and Pompeii, and museums — **Uffizi** (Florence), **Louvre** (Paris), **British Museum** (London). "Seeing cultural relics in person is like experiencing relevant histories."
- The contrast: **in traditional China, valuable objects were in the hands of social elites and political powers** (imperial and private collections), not the public.
- His 1913 essay advocated: **preserving art and historical relics; building museums.**
- Benefits he listed:
  1. cultivate people's aesthetic sense and ability to appreciate art;
  2. cultivate temperament and develop wisdom;
  3. raise **national esteem and pride**;
  4. only then could China **stand up against foreign plunder** of its treasures (Eight-Nation Alliance, 1900).

### Impact 2 — Theory of Realism (1917) ★
- **Realism:** representing people, places and things in a believable, recognisable way — form and details, mass and volume, proportion, shading and illumination, perspective. Well practised in Europe (Raphael).
- Kang **"invented an indigenous history of realism in China"** as a **remedy for the deficiency of literati painting**:
  - Chinese painting of the **Tang and Song** dynasties was **the best in the world because of its realism** (Han Gan's *Night-Shining White*; the seated portrait of Song Emperor Renzong).
  - It **declined from the Yuan dynasty / 13th century** (contrast: Tang Yin's Ming-dynasty *Autumn Wind Fan*).
- **Adopted by his student Xu Beihong** (*Kang Youwei and His Family*, 1917).

## 3. Public exhibitions and museums ★
- **Chen Hengque, *Viewing Paintings* (1918):** shows a **1917 exhibition in Beijing's Central Park** raising funds for **flood victims**; 600–700 paintings from Beijing collections shown for seven days on rotation. Significance: **public viewership**; art joins **philanthropy**; reflects Cai Yuanpei's aesthetic education and social responsibility.
- **Palace Museum, Beijing, opened 1925:** public display of the **former Qing imperial collection**.

## 4. Cai Yuanpei (1868–1940) and aesthetic education ★
- Studied philosophy, psychology and art history at the **University of Leipzig, Germany** (1907–11).
- **Minister of Education** of the provisional Republican government (1912); **Chancellor of Peking University** (1916).
- Advocated **aesthetic education (*meiyu*)**.
- **"Replacing Religion with Aesthetic Education" (1917):** love of beauty eliminates greed and prejudice and frees minds from selfishness and hatred → a new way of perceiving reality → spiritual well-being → social harmony → **transforms China into a modern country**.
- **Methods:** art curriculum in higher education; **drawing from life**; **absorb the best elements of Western art into Chinese art**; research society on the future of Chinese art; museums and exhibitions.

## 5. Conclusion slides ★
- **Institutionalization of art as modernization: preservation, display and education.**
- Overseas-returned intellectuals played an important role.
- Kang: relics conservation + realism. Cai: aesthetic education; the ethical value of art.
- **Results:** art schools and societies burgeoned in major cities in the **1920s**; aesthetic education systematised; **Palace Museum** opened; **First National Art Exhibition, 1929**.

## Likely questions — Week 3
- What failed in 1898, and what happened to Kang? *Hundred Days' Reform; 16 years of exile.*
- What did Kang advocate after seeing European museums? *Preserving relics and building public museums.*
- Which was a claimed benefit of conservation? *National esteem and pride; resisting foreign plunder.*
- According to Kang, when was Chinese painting best and why? *Tang and Song; realism.* When did it decline? *From the Yuan.*
- Who adopted Kang's realism? *Xu Beihong.*
- Who proposed replacing religion with aesthetic education? *Cai Yuanpei, 1917.*
- What did Chen Hengque's *Viewing Paintings* record? *A 1917 public charity exhibition for flood victims.*
- What did the Palace Museum display in 1925? *The former Qing imperial collection, to the public.*
- What three activities sum up institutionalization? *Preservation, display, education.*

# Week 4 — Republican Era: Chinese Painting, Travel and Nation Building

**Big idea:** In the Nanjing Decade the Nationalist government built railways and roads in the southeast, then used tours, publications and **guohua landscape painting** to turn that infrastructure into national identity. This is **cultural nationalism**.

## 1. Historical background
- **1911–12:** revolution led by **Sun Yat-sen** (1866–1925); the young emperor **Puyi** abdicated in 1912; Qing overthrown; Republic of China established in Beijing.
- **Warlord period (1912–1927):** fragmented government, constant turnover; mid-1920s many Shanghai residents fled to the foreign concessions; the cultural world was **largely unsupported by government**.
- ★ **Nanjing Decade (1928–1937):** the **Nationalist Party (Kuomintang, KMT / Guomindang, GMD)** set up government in **Nanjing** (1927–28) and consolidated power in the early 1930s by driving the CCP out of the southeast. The northeast was taken by Japan and the USSR.
- A **decade of nation-building / nationalization**: higher education (**National Central University**, Nanjing), art and culture (curricula, academies, exhibitions, societies), and **infrastructure — roads and railways as a high priority**.

## 2. Infrastructure construction ★
- The **National Economic Council (NEC)** ran a project in **five southeastern provinces: Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Anhui, Jiangxi** — a network of railways and highways linking new routes to existing ones.
- **Why? Four strategic reasons:**

| Reason | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Unification** | Unify a fragmented nation; symbol of Nationalist **sovereignty** over the southeast |
| **Accessibility** | Open up previously inaccessible scenic sites (Zhejiang: picturesque mountains, poor transport; e.g. Yandang Mountain) |
| **Commodification** | Turn sites into commodities through **tourism** → economic development |
| **Identity** | Foster a modern **national identity** (e.g. West Lake) |

## 3. Key definitions ★
- **Nationalism:** an ideology based on the premise that the individual's loyalty and devotion to the nation-state surpass other individual or group interests.
- **Cultural nationalism:** the mobilization of culture as a way to construct a national identity.

## 4. The Southeast Scenic Tour (東南交通周覽會) ★
- A series of scenic tours organised by the **Department of Reconstruction, Zhejiang provincial government**.
- Purposes: let participants **experience the new railways and roads**; **make the infrastructure visible**; **bring the hinterland into view**.
- **Three routes, all starting from Hangzhou.**
- **Phase I:** politicians, journalists, writers and artists → post-tour **exhibition and catalogue**. **Phase II:** open to the public.

## 5. Guohua ★
- *Guo* = national, *hua* = painting. **The nationalization of ink-and-brush painting as "the Chinese national painting".**
- **Traditionally** painting was classified **by subject matter** — landscape, bird-and-flower, figure — and was an **elite** art.
- **In the modern era** it is classified **by nation**: **guohua (Chinese painting, a national art) vs xihua (Western painting)**.
- Why landscape in particular? The **revivalist assertion** (early 1930s): landscape painting is the very foundation of China's greatest artistic tradition and will remain the central genre.
- **Zheng Wuchang**, *Chinese Painting Monthly* (Dec 1934): China lags behind militarily, politically and economically; **"the only thing about which we can be proud is our culture"**; painters should carry out **cultural construction and cultural preservation**.

## 6. *In Search of the Southeast* (東南攬勝, 1935) ★
- A **state-funded anthology** published after the tour by the Southeastern Infrastructure Tour Propaganda Group of the NEC.
- Contents: **travel literature, poems, landscape photography, Chinese painting**, plus location, route and topographic maps; chapters follow routes along trans-provincial highways and railways.
- ★ The paintings **depict the scenic sites in a traditional Chinese way — mountain (*shan*) and water (*shui*) — without any trace of modern touristic activity.**
- **He Tianjian, *The Fourth Cataract of the Five Cataracts in Zhuji* (1934):** combines **monumentality of Northern Song landscape** with **calligraphic brushwork of Yuan and Ming landscape**; **texture strokes**.
- Wang Jiqian (C. C. Wang), *Xiansheng Gate* (1934).

## 7. What it all means ★
- Nationalist political ideology: **revitalise traditional Chinese culture** and **modernise Chinese travel culture**; painters and government both took part.
- **Guohua = the representative art form of modern China** ("Chineseness", geographical mobility, modernity); Chinese national and cultural identity; **a tool for cultural propaganda, locally and internationally**.

## Likely questions — Week 4
- When was the Nanjing Decade and who governed? *1928–37, the Nationalist Party.*
- What did the Nationalists prioritise in nation-building? *Infrastructure: roads and railways.*
- Which five provinces? *Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Anhui, Jiangxi.*
- Four reasons for transport building? *Unification, accessibility, commodification, identity.*
- Who organised the Southeast Scenic Tour and where did routes start? *Zhejiang Department of Reconstruction; Hangzhou.*
- Who went in Phase I? *Politicians, journalists, writers, artists.*
- What is guohua? *Ink-and-brush painting nationalised as Chinese national painting; opposite of xihua.*
- How was painting classified traditionally vs in the modern era? *By subject matter vs by nation.*
- How did the anthology's paintings show the sites? *Traditional mountain-and-water, no trace of modern tourism.*
- Define cultural nationalism. *Mobilising culture to construct a national identity.*

# Week 5 — Republican Era: Artistic Searches for Modernity

**Big idea:** Two painters trained at the same Paris academy came home with **opposite** answers to "how do we modernise Chinese art?" — **Xu Beihong (academic realism, art for society)** and **Lin Fengmian (modernism, art for art's sake)**. One of her two sample questions comes from here.

## 1. Definitions ★
- **Modernization:** the **process** of transforming, a break from the past, from traditional/rural/agrarian to secular/urban/industrial.
- **Modernity:** the **quality or state** of being or appearing modern, a break from the past as a result of **industrialization, urbanization and the use of technology**.
- Examples: rickshaw → tramway; Forbidden City → Palace Museum (1925); painted portrait of Empress Dowager Ci'an → photographs of Chinese ladies.

## 2. Background
- First Sino-Japanese War → Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895); 1911 Revolution; warlord period (1912–27).
- **Studying abroad** (early 20th century), funded by **personal funds or government sponsorship**: Chen Duxiu, Lu Xun, Gao Jianfu, Yu Dafu, Guo Moruo (Japan); Cai Yuanpei (Germany); Hu Shi, Liang Sicheng (USA); Xu Zhimo (USA, then UK); Zhou Enlai (Japan, France); Deng Xiaoping (France).

## 3. Paris: two sectors ★

| | **Art Academy** | **Modern Art** |
|---|---|---|
| When | Popular across Western Europe since the **18th century** | Emerged in France in the **late 19th century** |
| Subjects | History, real people and life, fables | — |
| Look | **Highly realistic and naturalistic** (imitates nature); **physiognomy and anatomy; chiaroscuro; three-dimensionality** | **Liberation of visual language instead of imitating reality** |
| Example | École nationale supérieure des beaux-arts; Jacques-Louis David, *Napoleon at the Saint-Bernard Pass* (1801) | Matisse, *Woman with a Hat* (1905), *Dance* (1910) |

Compare them on: line, colour, light and shading, space, three-dimensionality.

## 4. Xu Beihong (1895–1953) ★
- Native of Yixing, Jiangsu. **1916:** French Department, Fudan University; met and studied under **Kang Youwei**. **1917:** studied art in Japan. **1919:** government scholarship to the **École nationale supérieure des beaux-arts, Paris**. Teacher: **Dagnan-Bouveret**, a French academic painter.
- Returned to China (slides: 1926); one of very few Chinese artists with a formal degree from that academy.
- Career: South China Arts Academy; **National Central University, Nanjing**.
- ★ **Approach: European academic curriculum — plaster casts, live models, still-life painting; copying old masterpieces.**
- ★ **Art theory (1927):** "Artists, like scientists, are guided by the **search for truth**. Just as mathematics is the basis of science, **figure drawing provides the foundation for art**." "The artist's responsibility is to **represent the beauty of nature**."
- Keywords on his slides: **realistic sketching, truth-seeking, chiaroscuro, physiognomy, anatomy, three-dimensionality, foreshortening.**

| Work | What to remember |
|---|---|
| *Kang Youwei and His Family* (1917), *He Zhanli* (Kang's third wife) | Early realism under Kang |
| *Drawing of a Female Nude* (1924), self-portraits | Chiaroscuro, anatomy, three-dimensionality |
| *Sound of the Flute* (1926, oil) | **Emptiness**: combines traditional Chinese painting aesthetics with Western oil painting (compare Ni Zan) |
| ***Tian Heng and His 500 Retainers*** (1928–30, oil, 198 × 355 cm) | **1. History painting × nationalism.** Late-Qin leader defeated by Han founder Liu Bang; flees to an island; kills himself rather than surrender; all 500 followers follow in loyalty. Models: David, Delacroix |
| *Awaiting the Deliverer* (1933, oil) | History painting |
| ***The Foolish Old Man Removes the Mountains*** (1940, ink and colour) | **2. Fable × nationalism.** Live models; a champion of **"new guohua"** |
| Horses (sketches 1928; *Horse in Ink* 1939, 1944) | **3. Horse × nationalism.** Realistic sketching from many angles, foreshortening |

## 5. Lin Fengmian (1900–1991) ★★ (sample question)
- Grew up in a rural village near Guangzhou. **1918:** also studied at the École in Paris.
- **Strong interest in the expressive power of Post-Impressionism, Fauvism and German Expressionism.**
- **1925: invited by Cai Yuanpei** to return as principal of Peking Art College. **1928:** co-founded the **Hangzhou National Academy of Art** (now China Academy of Art); director and professor.
- ★ **Sample answer: "Liberation of forms, color, and lines."**

| Strand | Work | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **1. German Expressionism** | *Suffering / Pain / Agony* (1929, oil, destroyed); compare Emil Nolde's *Crucifixion* | **To comment on social reality; to express a modern consciousness** (persecution of the CCP) |
| **2. Fauvism — the nude** | *Nude* (1934); compare Matisse | The nude as **a symbol of the West, of the modern, and of freedom** |
| **3. Lyricism** | *Riverside* (1938); compare Nolde's *The Evening* | **Combining Chinese and Western aesthetics** |

## 6. Final slide ★
**Two ways of modernizing Chinese painting — Xu Beihong: art for social and political sake. Lin Fengmian: art for art's sake.**

## Likely questions — Week 5
- Difference between modernization and modernity? *Process vs quality/state.*
- Characteristics of academy art? *Realistic, anatomy, chiaroscuro, three-dimensionality.* Of modern art? *Liberation of visual language.*
- Where did both Xu and Lin study? *École nationale supérieure des beaux-arts, Paris.*
- Xu's teaching method? *Plaster casts, live models, still life, copying masterpieces.*
- Xu's view of the artist? *Like a scientist seeking truth; figure drawing is the foundation.*
- What does *Tian Heng* combine? *History painting and nationalism (loyalty, refusal to surrender).*
- Which movements interested Lin? *Post-Impressionism, Fauvism, German Expressionism.*
- Who invited Lin back, and what did he found? *Cai Yuanpei; Hangzhou National Academy of Art, 1928.*
- What did the nude symbolise? *The West, the modern, freedom.*
- Art for art's sake? *Lin.* Art for social and political sake? *Xu.*

# Week 6 — Republican Era: The Birth of Avant-Garde in China

**Big idea:** In the 1930s two very different groups stood "at the front" against stale art. The **Storm Society** wanted a pure world of colour, line and form. The **Modern Woodcut Movement** wanted art that showed collective suffering and struggle.

## 1. The avant-garde ★
- French: *avant* = front, *garde* = guard.
- **Characteristics: iconoclastic** (strongly opposing accepted beliefs and traditions), **individualistic, self-expressive, idealistic.**
- Media: oil painting, woodcut print, design. Cities: **Shanghai, Hangzhou, Beijing, Guangzhou.**

## 2. The trigger
- **First National Exhibition, Shanghai, 1929.**
- Artists were dissatisfied with the **official construction of certain practices as the recognised style** → **proliferation of art societies of all kinds.**

## 3. The Storm Society (Juelan she), 1931–1935 ★
- *Juelan* = "breaking wave": **"Hit the rotten art of contemporary China with a powerful wave."**
- **Based in Shanghai.** Members were **painters trained in Japan and France** who had returned: **Ni Yide (Japan)**, **Pang Xunqin (France)**.
- **Advocated art for art's sake; reformists introducing a radical notion of modernism.**
- ★ **Manifesto (October 1932):** "Our entire art world today is decadent and diseased… With our raging passion and iron intellect, **we will create a world interwoven with color, line, and form!**"
- **Four exhibitions, 1932–1935**, showing work inspired by contemporary European trends; impact diminished from 1933.

**Ni Yide (1901–70)** — studied in **Japan twice** (returned 1922, then 1928 after exposure to more avant-garde European trends); taught at Shanghai Art Academy; aimed "to remake the Chinese art world". *Summer* (1932), *Moonlit Night* (1945). His work is contrasted with Xu Beihong's *Tian Heng*.

**Pang Xunqin (1906–85)** — prosperous Jiangsu family; first studied **medicine** at a French-language university in Shanghai; left for **Paris at 19**; returned **1930**; taught, opened a private art workshop, **designed advertisements and book covers**.

| Work | What to remember |
|---|---|
| *Such is Paris* (1931) | **Collage: fragmentation, juxtaposition** (compare Picasso, 1913) |
| *Such is Shanghai* (1934) | Fragmented images of **materialistic urban life**; emphasis on money |
| *Composition* (1934, destroyed) | **Surrealism** (alternative realities, dream worlds, the unconscious; compare Dalí). **A robot and a Chinese peasant girl**, industrial machinery, joined in **dehumanizing bondage** |
| *Son of the Earth* (1934, watercolour) | A **dying child** of a Jiangnan peasant family during a devastating **drought**; shows his growing concern for people's hardship |

## 4. Woodcut print ★
- A **printmaking technique**: an image printed from a carved wooden plate. Tools: **knives, chisels, gouges** (not a brush). **Raised areas** are inked and print; **recessed areas** stay blank.
- **Characteristics:**
  - **Visual power:** sharp contrast of black ink and uninked paper.
  - **Reproducible:** public service.
  - **Propagation of ideas.**
  - **Less expensive** than oil painting: good for poor young artists.
- An **international** art form in the early 20th century, pursued by **left-wing, young, progressive** artists to **reveal oppressive social reality**: the aftermath of WWI and **underrepresented groups whose presence and demands had not been acknowledged**.
- **Käthe Kollwitz**, *War* series (1921–23): *The Volunteers*, *The Widow I*, *The Mothers* — those left behind (mothers, widows, children).

## 5. Modern Woodcut Movement in China (1930s) ★
- **Introduced and propelled by Lu Xun (1881–1936):** trained in Japan; switched from science and **medicine to literature**; **father of modern Chinese literature** and **father of the modern woodcut movement in China**; interested in left-wing art and political theory.
- **No manifesto.** Started in **Shanghai**, spread to Hangzhou, Beijing and **Guangzhou (a late epicentre)**. Some artists had trained as **oil painters** in academies.
- **Powers:** raw, easy to read, powerful images; potential to enlighten the people; fast and cheap to reproduce → **an excellent medium of social activism**.
- **Used by the left wing:** artists were **arrested by the Nationalist government, charged with "endangering the Republic"**, and imprisoned.

| Work | What to remember |
|---|---|
| **Jiang Feng, *Kill the Resisters* (1931)** | Nationalist troops aim rifles at a crowd; demonstrators flee in terror |

## 6. Conclusion slides ★
- **The avant-garde:** stands at the forefront to propel change; resists stale representationalism; multiple origins in modern China.
- **Storm Society — interest:** the spirit of the modern age, Westernised. **Approach:** creating a pure formal world; **liberating lines, colours and shapes**.
- **Modern Woodcut Movement — interest:** modern life as a **collective struggle for a better future**. **Approach:** representing **underrepresented groups**.
- **Zeitgeist:** the spirit of an era; social reality or the truth about the current age.

# Week 7 — Republican Era: Art and Warfare

**Big idea:** During the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–45) China split into three zones. Art took different roles in each: **recording reality, raising morale and shaping opinion, and raising money and foreign support.**

## 1. The war and the map ★
- **Second Sino-Japanese War, 1937–1945.** **Nanjing Massacre: December 1937 – January 1938.**
- **Nationalist government moved inland: Nanjing → Wuhan → Chongqing.**
- Ordinary people: stay in coastal cities; move inland/southwest (**Chongqing, Guilin, Kunming**); go to the Communist stronghold **Yan'an**; Hong Kong, Macau; Southeast Asia; Europe and North America.

| Zone | Centre | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Japanese-controlled** | Nanjing, Beijing, Shanghai | **Puppet government in Nanjing under Wang Jingwei**, collaborating with Japan. Shanghai's foreign concessions kept limited autonomy |
| **Nationalist-controlled** | **Wartime capital Chongqing**; Guilin; Dunhuang | — |
| **Communist-controlled** | **Yan'an** | — |

- **Second United Front:** collaboration between the Nationalists (GMD/KMT) and the CCP to resist Japan; by **early 1941** the two increasingly turned against each other.

## 2. Nationalist-controlled areas

**Chongqing — Huang Junbi (1898–1991), *Bombing of Chongqing, June 11, 1939*** ★
- A **guohua professor** who moved with the **National Central University** from Nanjing to Chongqing; close to the Nationalists; **relocated to Taiwan after 1949**.
- **Landscape painting** traditionally served the elite and was philosophical. Here it has a **reportage function: a specific location and time**.
- **Ink washes and red pigment** mimic smoke and fire.

**Guilin — Cai Dizhi, *Fleeing Guilin by the North Station* (1944, woodcut)**
- Cai was a student at Guangzhou Municipal Art School who moved to Guilin in 1943.
- Refugees fleeing as Japanese forces reached the region in 1944. **Shading and black-white contrast → desperation and terror.** Compare Jiang Feng's *Kill the Resisters*.

**Chongqing — Ding Cong, *Images of Today* (1945, ink and gouache handscroll)** ★
- Ding Cong was a **cartoonist**.
- Figures: intellectual, merchant, painter, soldier, official, profiteer, journalist; arranged as five groups of class activity.
- **Social and political satire: corruption, bureaucratism, wasteful hoarding, poverty.**

**Dunhuang — Zhang Daqian (1899–1983)** ★
- Dunhuang: **northern Gansu**, desert on the border of Central Asia and China; a **Silk Routes** trading town; a **Buddhist centre of worship** (5th–14th c.) with cave temples and **murals**. **Rediscovered in the 20th century**; seen as **China's native artistic culture**.
- Zhang, a renowned painter from **Sichuan**, **stayed 2.5 years (1941–43) and copied 276 murals**.
- *Portrait of Guanyin from Dunhuang Fresco* (1943): ornate halo, jewellery, lotus seat; imitates the murals' imagery, style and technique; **the art of long-lost, rediscovered historical epochs**. (Sold for HKD 48 million at Sotheby's, 2019.)

## 3. Japanese-controlled areas

**Jiang Zhaohe (1904–1986), *Refugees* (1943, ink and colour on paper)** ★
- Taught himself Western oil painting (1920); taught at National Central University (1928).
- **Originally a huge scroll, 2 m high and 26 m long; life-sized; more than a hundred figures.**
- **Portrays Chinese people's suffering under the Japanese occupation.**
- Shown in **Beijing: forced to close**. Later shown in Shanghai: "borrowed" by the Japanese, **half of it lost**.
- Drawn from **self-hired life models**. **Chinese ink wash + European realism.**
- Shows **the poor, the vulnerable, the disadvantaged; solicits empathy from the viewer.**

## 4. The international sphere — to solicit political or financial support

**Zhang Shuqi (1901–1957), *Messengers of World Peace* (1940)** ★
- Guohua professor at National Central University. A **bird-and-flower painting** (doves): a traditional subject.
- **A diplomatic gift to arouse American sympathy** for the exiled Nationalist regime and its people; inspired US support against Japan.

**Xu Beihong, *Four Horses* (1940)** ★
- From **1939** a long **fundraising trip to Singapore and India**.
- The horse was popular with the **Southeast Asian diaspora**; it symbolises the **noble and heroic spirit of his nation**.

## 5. Conclusion slides ★
- **Roles of art in wartime:** **capture social realities; raise morale and affect public opinion; generate funds to support the war effort.**
- **Methods:** **realism; satire in cartoon; deployment of the Chinese cultural heritage; symbolism and allegory.**

Matching methods to works: realism → Jiang Zhaohe; satire → Ding Cong; cultural heritage → Zhang Daqian at Dunhuang; symbolism and allegory → Zhang Shuqi's doves, Xu Beihong's horses.
