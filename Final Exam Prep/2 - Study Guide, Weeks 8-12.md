# Week 8 — Mao Era: Art and Socialist Nation-Building I

> **Source note.** The Week 8 PowerPoint is missing from your folder. **Big idea:** After 1949 art had to serve politics. Every medium — New Year pictures, propaganda posters, oil painting — joined the nation-building project, guided by **socialist realism**.

## A. First half (outline — verify on Canvas)

- ★ **"Politics in command": art has to serve political purposes.**
  - First set out comprehensively by **Mao Zedong in 1942**, in a series of meetings (talks) at **Yan'an**, the CCP's wartime stronghold. At first practised mainly by leftist artists.
  - After the **PRC was founded in 1949 (capital Beijing)** and the Nationalists were driven from the mainland, it applied to art and culture **comprehensively**.
- **Social remodelling:** from a capitalist model that privileged elites to a socialist society meant to erase class divides and hierarchy; **land reform** and collectivization.
- **New Year pictures (*nianhua*):** a traditional folk art for the Chinese New Year (deities, auspicious motifs). The new state launched a **New Nianhua movement in 1949** to carry socialist messages to the widest audience, including the illiterate.
- ★ **Propaganda poster:** combines an **image** with a **political slogan** to promote state ideas and political or economic initiatives. **A category of visual medium introduced from the Soviet Union.**
  - How they were made: painters of every specialism (Chinese painting, oil, watercolour) painted an individual work, which an **art publishing house** then turned into a poster for **mass production**.
- ★ **Iconography:** depicting specific facial features, clothing and associated motifs so viewers can immediately identify who a figure is.

## B. Socialist realism ★★ — "I would definitely ask about socialist realism"

**What it is**
- **An ideological guidance — "not specifically a style" but a set of doctrines** guiding art producers.
- Tied to **socialism and the Soviet Union**. Used in the USSR from **1934 to the 1980s**.
- Known earlier to leftist artists, but **comprehensively introduced and enforced in China in 1953**.
- Why "realism"? So that **everyone — intellectuals, farmers, workers — can read the image at once**. **Abstraction** was associated with the capitalist West, so realism marked an **ideological divide**.

**The four guiding principles** (memorise)

| # | Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Realistic** | Recognisable figures, correct proportion, volume, space, perspective |
| 2 | **Proletarian subjects** | Peasants, workers (and soldiers), not the bourgeois elite |
| 3 | **Typical scenes of people's daily life** | Show people in their representative social role: workers in factories, farmers in fields |
| 4 | **Idealistic** | Show the ideal, beyond the real: positive, healthy, hopeful |

**The three functions** (also on the conclusion slide)
1. **Exemplify** communist values (equality, no class divide).
2. **Educate** the public (through exhibitions, newspapers, magazines).
3. **Instil** a sense of hope and optimism — a bright future under communism.

**Result:** art immediately serves **political and propaganda** purposes. In the 1950s figures are given a **healthy, strong, heroic** look. Many artists found the change difficult because art had not carried that ideological function before.

## C. Three posters she analysed

**1. *Long Live Chairman Mao* (1957)** ★
- A **mother holding her daughter**, both smiling, looking to the upper corner where a **Huabiao column** is faintly shown — the ornamental column in front of **Tiananmen Gate**. They stand on **Chang'an Avenue**, where national parades pass, on the morning of the **National Day** parade. Flowers and ribbons fly around them; light falls on their faces.
- Against the four principles: **realistic** style; **ordinary people**; scene is **less "everyday"** (a parade day) but still ordinary people's life; strongly **idealistic** — happiness, light and flowers = hope under Mao and the Party.

**2. *Soviet experts visit a rural commune*** ★
- A greenhouse; a nicely dressed **young peasant girl shows a ripe tomato to a Soviet expert** (long coat, hat, shiny shoes, camera); a translator; more Soviet experts and women peasants behind.
- Peasants look **healthy, round-faced, rosy, happy** → **a good harvest**.
- Message: **under socialist leadership** (redistribution, nationalization, collectivization, land reform) **farmers enjoy good harvests**, helped by **Soviet expert advice**.
- A **rural commune** = a way of organising peasants in an area to farm together.

**3. *We and ore together make steel!* (Hubei People's Publishing; slide date January 1959)** ★
- Context: the **Great Leap Forward (1958–60)**, Mao's campaign to speed up agricultural and industrial production. Slogan: **"Greater, faster, better, and more economical."**
- **Backyard furnaces:** everyone was urged to make steel with low-tech home-built furnaces, donating household metal. The product was poor-quality **pig iron**.
- The poster: a young woman and man jointly **hold up a lump of pig iron**. Their shirts show they are **medical students from Wuhan** — university students were pulled out of class to make steel. Background: an ideological lecture and a backyard furnace.
- Placed on the **central vertical axis**, in the foreground, filling half the sheet; **young, healthy, optimistic, proud**.
- Against the principles: realistic; ordinary people (students joining the state campaign); **idealistic** — proud and happy even though the steel was poor.
- The campaign itself ended in **famine** (the video she showed said 30–50 million deaths).

*Also mentioned:* the website **chineseposters.net** and the **New Marriage Law (1950)** posters. Background, unlikely to be tested.

## D. Dong Xiwen, *Inaugural Ceremony for the New Nation* (1952–53, oil on canvas) ★★

Also translated *The Founding of the Nation*.

**What it shows**
- **1 October 1949**: Mao on **Tiananmen Gate** proclaiming the PRC and its new government. Below: **Chang'an Avenue**, later Tiananmen Square.
- Made for the PRC's early anniversaries; displayed in the museum and **mass-reproduced as posters, in newspapers and magazines** → **an icon of the new Chinese nation**.

**Who is in it**
- **Mao** at the prominent centre.
- Front row from the left: **Zhu De** (head of the People's Liberation Army), **Liu Shaoqi**, **Song Qingling** (wife of Sun Yat-sen), and others including **Gao Gang** (wearing glasses).
- **Zhou Enlai** (Premier), far left of the second row.

**Real but not real** ★ (her comparison with the newsreel)
- Real: the specific date, place, crowd, lanterns.
- Manipulated: in film and photographs leaders crowd round Mao and he is hard to pick out. In the painting **Mao stands apart with clear space around him**, leaders in neat rows looking respectfully at him → **hierarchy is obvious at once**.
- **Blue sky fills about a third of the picture** = hope, a bright future.
- Lesson: **even a realist painting can rearrange positions, expressions and sky to carry a message.**

**Sinicization of oil painting** ★★
- **Sinicization = turning something that came from elsewhere into something more Chinese.** Oil painting was seen as a medium from Europe and America; for nation-building it had to look Chinese.
- How Dong Xiwen did it — **Chinese architectural and decorative motifs**:
  - **red lanterns**
  - **red columns / ornate woodwork**
  - **marble parapet (balustrade)**
  - **porcelain flower pots**
  - **silk carpet with traditional Chinese cloud motifs**
  - **yellow roof tiles**
- **Grandeur:** space opens from the platform across the parapet and iron railing to the broad avenue lined with flags.

**The versions — painting as Party history** ★★

| Version | Change | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Original, 1952–53** | — | — |
| Revision | **Gao Gang removed** | Gao Gang was **purged in 1954–55** |
| **Third version, 1967** | **Liu Shaoqi removed** | Liu was **purged in 1967**, at the start of the Cultural Revolution |
| Later version (1970s) | **Liu Shaoqi restored**; Gao Gang still absent | Liu's standing was restored |

- **Purge:** removal of unwanted figures from the Party, for "wrongdoing" or ideology that did not align. Once purged, a person was **erased visually too — erased from the nation's memory**.
- **Why Liu Shaoqi?** After the Great Leap Forward's failure Mao stepped back and Liu led in the early 1960s with a **moderate, gradual** approach against Mao's **radical** one. When Mao regained power in the mid-1960s, his rival had to go.
- **How Liu was painted out:** Song Qingling's shoulder was extended; Liu's body became that of the man in the second row; part of his head was covered with blue.
- **Other changes in the 1967 version:**
  - **Two more microphones added** → pulls attention to the centre, away from the altered left side.
  - **Clouds removed, more blue sky** → forms a **halo-like** shape around Mao's head, like a sacred figure → heightens his **political sanctity**.
  - Colour pushed redder.
- **Why this was possible:** **oil paint is thick and opaque**, applied in layers, so earlier work can be covered. Chinese **ink is thin, translucent and water-based** and hard to correct.

## E. Great Leap Forward — what to know
- **1958–60**, initiated by Mao; **collectivization and industrialization at once**.
- Moderates (Zhou Enlai, Liu Shaoqi) wanted a gradual transition.
- Agriculture was meant to feed the cities and support industrial growth.
- Outcome: **humanitarian and economic disaster; the Great Famine**; Mao stepped back.

## F. Conclusion slide ★
- Period: **1949 to the early 1960s** — the role of art in Chinese socialist nation-building.
- **All visual media** (New Year pictures, propaganda posters, painting) took part.
- Techniques: **iconography**; **sinicization of oil painting**.
- **Socialist realism: to exemplify, to educate, to instil.** Figures look healthy, strong, heroic.
- In the **early 1960s**, with a changed political atmosphere, **"sweet and lyrical" subjects** were allowed.

# Week 10 — Mao Era: Art and the Cultural Revolution

**Big idea:** The Cultural Revolution (1966–76) grew out of a power struggle after the Great Leap Forward. Its visual culture — big-character posters, Red Guard art, Mao portraits, worker-peasant-soldier art — **mobilised emotion and action, glorified leaders and ordinary people, and imagined an ideal society.**

## 1. What it was ★
- **1966–1976.** Also called the **Great Cultural Revolution** or the **Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution**.
- **A complex social upheaval mobilised among university and high school students, calling for "continuing revolution" through revolutionary struggle.**
- **Educated urban youth were sent down to the countryside** for manual labour.
- The first four years were the most intense.

## 2. How it happened ★
- It **began as a political struggle between Chairman Mao and other top Party leaders for dominance of the CCP**, then spread to all walks of life.
- Traceable to the **Great Leap Forward (1958–60)**, Mao's radical socio-economic movement:
  - **Industrialization of people's work** (machines, tractors, tools);
  - **Collectivization of people's lives** (land + labour + time; marching to the fields together; communal canteens);
  - aim: speed up agricultural and industrial production; slogan **"Greater, faster, better, and more economical!"**;
  - result: **catastrophe — great famine, more than 30 million dead**.
- **Early 1960s:** **Mao stepped down from governing the country. Vice-Chairman Liu Shaoqi and Premier Zhou Enlai stepped up** — more practical, moderate leaders with **more conservative policies based on individual incentives**, e.g. **letting families farm their own plots**. **The economy grew steadily, 1962–65.**
- ★ **Mao detested these policies as a sign of private ownership**, against **pure communism** → **launched the Cultural Revolution in 1966**.

## 3. Red Guards ★
- **Schools and universities closed in autumn and winter 1966.**
- **University and high school students formed militant units** — the front line of the upheaval. Student life was **militarised**.
- **Armbands** (red cloth) were distributed by radical leaders, declaring students "Red Guards" able to take part in revolutionary struggle. They ranged from moderate to ultra-patriotic.
- **Little Red Book:** a collection of **Mao's sayings** for Red Guards to study and follow; pocket-sized.

## 4. Big-character poster — *dazibao* ★★ ("remember the name")
- *Da* = big, *zi* = character, *bao* = poster.
- **A handwritten big poster used to attack targeted enemies** — at first higher administrators in schools and government offices.
- **Began at Peking University**, then spread to other universities, high schools and government offices.
- Targets: **authority figures**, attacked **verbally and physically** — Party members, teachers, administrators, neighbours, **even friends, relatives and parents**.
- Accusations: **"rightist"** thinking, experience or connections (e.g. relatives overseas); **"counter-revolutionary"** behaviour.
- Victims wore dunce caps and placards with their names crossed out.
- **Purges** in the high ranks of the Party ran at the same time: **Lu Dingyi**, **Peng Zhen**, **Liu Shaoqi (1967)**.
- **Binary thinking: "You cross out the bad person and leave the good person."**
- Link back: Liu Shaoqi's removal from Dong Xiwen's painting (third version, 1967).

## 5. The Four Olds ★★
- **Old customs, old habits, old culture, old thinking.**
- Temples, works of art, buildings and even museum objects were smashed.
- Example: a Buddha at **Lingyin Temple, Hangzhou**, covered with signs: "Destroy the old world", "Establish a new world", "Long live the proletariat in power!"

## 6. Red Guard art ★
- Posters of PLA soldiers and Red Guards charging forward with the Little Red Book and weapons.
- **Woodcut Red Guard poster (1966): "Revolution is no crime, rebellion is justified."**
- **Model opera:** actors wear Red Guard-style armbands and carry guns; **poses and hand gestures modified from Peking Opera** — which is why they look so forceful.
- ★ ***Scatter the Old World, Build a New World* (April 1967)**, by the **Central Art Academy Woodblock Print Combat Group** (art students organised into production teams):
  - a Red Guard (uniform, armband) swings a huge **hammer** at the Four Olds: a **Buddha statue, a crucifix, old books, a vinyl record, dice**, a moral-teaching inscription;
  - background: a state-run cinema ("The East is Red") with a red star and flag; Red Guards marching with banners; small panels suggesting dazibao;
  - notes on the slide: Red Guards **storm buildings and take down signboards**; property of "rightists" and anything **bourgeois, decadent, old or religious** is smashed.
  - Class discussion point: the **Nationalists used traditional culture** to build a nation (Week 4), the **Cultural Revolution erased it** to build a proletarian culture.
- ★ ***Fully Criticize the Chinese Khrushchev…* (November 1967):**
  - **Khrushchev** = Soviet leader, **Stalin's successor**, who **denounced Stalin's misdeeds** (slide: 1955) and was eventually dismissed and put under house arrest.
  - **Liu Shaoqi = "the Chinese Khrushchev"**: Mao's successor after the Great Leap Forward who "betrayed Mao's policies".
  - Figures: a **worker** (cap, neck-cloth, work trousers) **holding a brush as a weapon**; a **peasant** (straw hat); a **soldier** (star on cap); a younger **Red Guard** (Mao badge), holding Mao's writings. Together = **the proletariat plus the Red Guards** attacking a figure hidden behind a crumbling structure.
  - Related posters: "hold the pen like a weapon" against **Liu** and **Deng** (Deng Xiaoping). She said the two extra posters need not be memorised.

## 7. Maoism and Mao as icon ★
- **Maoism: a doctrine composed of the ideology and methodology for revolution developed by Mao Zedong and his associates in the CCP from the 1920s until Mao's death in 1976.**
- Spread through the Little Red Book and anthologies, inside China and across the **Third World**: Mao as an international leader.
- Mao's image was reproduced across **all media**: oil painting, woodcut, Chinese painting, poster, photograph, sculpture.

| Work | What to remember |
|---|---|
| Poster of Mao (1966–76, British Museum) | Slogan "Long live the great leader Chairman Mao"; based on a **1925 photograph of Mao in Guangzhou**; printed in millions |
| ★ **Liu Chunhua and others, *Chairman Mao Goes to Anyuan* (1968, oil, 2.2 × 1.8 m)** | **Young Mao**; figure about **life-size** on the **central vertical axis**; low viewpoint makes him tall; wide landscape. **History painting of leaders learned from the Soviet Union** (compare Rylov, *Lenin in Razliv*, 1934). Process: **photograph → gridded sketch → oil painting → poster**; joint collaboration, **censorship**, mass-produced nationwide |
| ★ **Yangtze swim** | Photograph by Mao's personal photographer of his **1966 swim in the Yangzi River**, aged over 70, to show his health. Poster *Closely follow the great leader Chairman Mao and forge ahead courageously amid great storms and waves* by the **Shanghai 1969 Swim the Yangzi River headquarters**, for the **third anniversary** (July 1969) |
| Henan 27 Commune poster (1967) | Propaganda poster reproduced from a woodcut |
| ★ **Tang Xiaohe, *Follow Closely Our Great Leader Chairman Mao…* (1972, oil)** | Mao on a boat, healthy, in sunlight, **surrounded by young people and Red Guards** (imagined, to show his popularity). Official style: **"red, bright, and shining"** |
| Sculptures of Mao | Colossal statues, arm raised; socialist realism |

## 8. Worker-Peasant-Soldier art and women ★
- Art that **celebrates ordinary people** — worker, peasant, soldier — as the **masters of the communist age**.
- **Feminism: liberation of women.** ★★ **Mao's slogan: "Women hold up half the sky."**
- Women were **emancipated** — no longer confined to home, childcare and housework — **and** needed: **"Emancipation + the great need for labor."**

| Work | What to remember |
|---|---|
| **Ding Hao, *We are proud of participating in the founding of our country's industrialization* (Feb 1954)** | A smiling woman worker with pushed-up helmet and tools in an industrial setting; women encouraged into **industrial and technical professions** |
| **Sun Quan, *Women hold up half the sky* (Nov 1974)** | Three women — workers with hammer and cable, and a **pilot** at the top — stacked into a monument rising to the sky, filling more than half the sheet; second printing ran to 173,000 |
| Oil painting | Learned from **Soviet socialist realism**: **Konstantin Maksimov** taught in **Beijing in the mid-to-late 1950s**; model workers, peasants, soldiers |
| **Yuan Hao, *Lofty Aspirations Touch the Clouds* (1973)** | A woman electrical worker alone on **high-voltage cables**, hardly any protective gear, smiling, river far below → heroism, no fear |
| **Chen Yanning, *New Barefoot Doctor in the Fishing Village* (1974)** | A **sent-down youth**: an urban girl with basic medical training, **barefoot** on a fishing boat, buckets marked with a red cross (herbal tea); sweaty, red-faced, happy, healthy |

## 9. Conclusion slide ★
**The role of art and visual materials:**
- **mobilising the viewer's emotion and their social actions;**
- **glorifying individuals, leaders, and ordinary people;**
- **imagining an idealistic society.**

# Week 11 — Post-Mao Era: Art and Post-Revolutionary China

**Big idea:** After Mao, artists stopped serving as a propaganda machine and **tested how much freedom the state would allow** — from inside the system (airport murals, New Realism) and from outside it (the Stars).

## 1. Post-revolutionary China ★
- **Defines the shift that began after Mao's death in 1976 and Deng Xiaoping's rise to power in 1978.** (Mao's named successor **Hua Guofeng** came between.)
- **End of continuous revolution:** under Mao the state prioritised **ongoing class struggle and ideological purity**; under Deng the Party **abandoned mass political campaigns in favour of economic modernization and stability**.
- **Integration into global capitalism:** Deng's **Open Door Policy** reconnected China's economy to the capitalist world.
- **Cultural and intellectual depoliticization:**
  - **Rightists and political prisoners were exonerated** and returned to positions of influence (**rehabilitated**).
  - **Sent-down educated youth returned** to the cities (1970 poster: "Go to the countryside, go to the border areas, go to the regions where the motherland needs you the most").
  - ★ **College admissions examination resumed in 1977**, open to anyone **younger than 31** and **not on a political blacklist**.
- ★ **Artistic reactions:**
  - criticised Mao's policies of the previous decade;
  - **overthrew socialist realism**;
  - **retold their vivid mental images of what they had actually seen**;
  - **pushed the freedom of artistic expression**.

## 2. Official art ★
**Official art = approved and sponsored by the state**, usually made by artists employed in state art academies.

**Beijing International Airport public art project**
- **Terminal 1 opened 1 January 1980**, to celebrate the **30th anniversary of the PRC**. (Terminal 2: 1 November 1999. Terminal 3: 2008, the Olympic year.)
- State buildings used murals to **decorate** and to **project an image of the nation** to travellers.

**Zhang Ding (1917–2010), *Nezha Conquers the Dragon King* (1979 mural)** ★
- Painter, cartoonist, muralist, calligrapher, craft designer; **professor at the Central Academy of Art and Design, Beijing**.
- **Purged in 1957 as a Rightist, again in the Cultural Revolution; rehabilitated in 1978.**
- **Artistic director of the animated film of the same name (1979, Shanghai Animation Film Studio)**, screened internationally (BBC Two, 1984).
- **Nezha:** a **warrior deity** of Chinese mythology, born after a **3.5-year** gestation, who saves his friends from the cruel **East Sea Dragon King**.
- ★ **Subject matter: national. Form: vivid colours, decorative style, traceable to the Dunhuang murals (national heritage).**
- He turned a **time-based film into a space-based mural**: Nezha appears several times across the wall.

**Yuan Yunsheng (b. 1937), *Water Splashing Festival* (1979)** ★
- Mural, **acrylic on canvas, 340 × 2,100 cm**, in the **former dining room** of the airport.
- **A holiday of the minority Dai people in Yunnan.**
- **Style: no longer Soviet socialist realism but an elongated figural style inspired by early-20th-century modern European art, especially Amedeo Modigliani.**
- ★ **The nude figures were controversial**: the state did not allow public display of nudity; the room was closed and the section hidden.
- Lesson: **a modern style was tolerated; nudity was not** — freedom was only partial.

## 3. Unofficial art: the Stars Group (1979) ★
**Unofficial art = not commissioned or approved by the state.**

- **Founded by Beijing-based underground artists and writers** who:
  - organised **poetry recitals at unsupervised parks**;
  - published **underground journals**;
  - expressed opinions at **Beijing's Democracy Wall**.
- Their application to exhibit at the **official Chinese National Art Gallery was not approved**.
- ★ **27 September 1979:** they hung their works **on the fence and trees outside** the gallery.
  - **More than 150 works by 23 artists**: woodcuts, oils, pencil drawings, guohua, **wooden sculptures**.
  - **Handmade** posters, object labels, tickets and exhibitors' badges.

**Wang Keping (b. 1949), wood sculptor** ★ — he works with the natural shape of the trunk and branches.

| Work | What to remember |
|---|---|
| ***Idol* (1979, wood, 57 cm)** | Blends the **iconography of Mao** (cap with star, fleshy face) with a **Buddha** (calm face, curved brows). One eye wide open, one half shut. **Post-revolutionary satire of Mao as the Communist star and political idol; critique of the cult of personality** |
| ***Silence* (1979)** | One eye **blocked** by a crossed plug, the **mouth stopped up** with a branch stub: people could not see clearly or speak freely |
| ***Chain* (1979)** | A **hand over the mouth**, a **chain around the neck**: lives under control |

**What happened** ★

| Day | Event |
|---|---|
| Day 1 (27 Sept) | **Rehabilitated senior art administrators appreciated** the artists' courage and ambition |
| Day 2 (28 Sept) | **Police intervened**, questioning legality |
| Day 3 (29 Sept) | **Police sealed off the site and confiscated the works** |
| Day 5 (**1 October, National Day**) | **Protest march of about 700 artists and supporters**, from the **Democracy Wall toward Tiananmen Square**; banners invoking the constitution and "art demands freedom" |
| Result | **Officially approved; reopened at Beihai Park in November.** A **second Stars show inside the National Art Gallery a year later** |

## 4. Academically trained oil painters: New Realism / Scar Art ★
Painters trained in realism now **turned realism upside down**: the same skill, no longer glorifying state policy, showing the real life of peasants and workers.

**Luo Zhongli, *Father* (1980, oil on canvas, 227 × 154 cm)** ★
- A huge, frontal, **larger-than-life** head of an old **peasant**: dark sun-burnt skin, deep wrinkles, dirt under the fingernails, a cloth on his head, holding a bowl.
- Compare the **1965 poster "The poor and lower-middle peasants are the masters of the people's commune"**: young, handsome, strong, smiling at harvest.
- Point: **no longer glorifying the peasant as master of the age; showing how harsh a farmer's life is.**

**Guang Tingbo (b. 1938, Dalian, Liaoning), *Molten Steel & Sweat* (1981)** ★
- **Oil painter of Manchu ethnicity**; graduated from the Oil Painting Department of the **Lu Xun Fine Arts Academy in 1964**; worked in the Liaoning branch of the Chinese Artists Association; moved to the Liaoning Painting Academy in 1980.
- A **steelworker** on a break, soaked in sweat, jacket off, drinking water, tired and dazed.
- Compare **"We and ore together make steel!" (1959)** and the **1977 Anshan Iron and Steel poster** (workers heroic, upright, full of energy).
- Point: **steelmaking is hard, exhausting work**, not a joyful heroic act. Both use realism; socialist realism adds mandatory optimism, New Realism removes it.

## 5. Take-away ★
- A **transitional period** from the Mao to the post-Mao era.
- Artists **inside and outside** the state system both **tested the limits** of what the state allowed.
- Some kept realism but changed its message; underground artists used **abstraction** and blunt satire about lived experience.
- (The last slide, "'85 Wave?", was a teaser and was not taught.)

- What did the 1 October march achieve? *Approval; reopening at Beihai Park; a second show a year later.*
- What does *Father* depict and how? *A peasant's hardship, unidealised, larger than life.*

# Week 12 — Post-Mao Era: Art and Globalization

**Big idea:** After the Cold War, capital, people and ideas moved freely. Chinese artists mixed Western contemporary techniques with Chinese content and experience, and had to decide **how to position themselves** in a Western-led art world. Two answers: **Political Pop** and the **global contemporary art space**.

*She said six questions come from this lecture.*

## 1. Globalization ★
- **Free flow of capital, human agents, and ideas around the world.**
- **Collaborations between institutions and governments of different countries.**
- → **a borderless world** → **the world as a global village** → **transcending the geopolitical boundaries imposed by the Cold War.**
- Mostly a **1990s–2000s** phenomenon. The Internet.

## 2. The Cold War order (1945–1991) ★
- **Geopolitical confrontation and isolation between capitalist and communist countries; division by ideology.**

| Bloc | Who |
|---|---|
| **Western Bloc** (capitalist) | Led by the USA; Western Europe, Canada, Australia… |
| **Eastern Bloc** (communist) | USSR, PRC, North Korea, North Vietnam, Cuba… |
| **Non-Aligned Movement** | Newly independent former colonies in Asia, Africa and South America that sided with **neither** bloc |

- **Berlin Wall:** divided East and West Berlin; **collapsed November 1989**.
- **End of the Cold War: collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 → capitalism led the world; capitalist globalization.**

## 3. China in the 1980s ★
- Opening (US–China diplomatic relations from 1972; Open Door Policy 1978) brought Western books, films and ideas; modern and contemporary art movements across China; performance and conceptual art (the tutorial's **Xiamen** group, who burned their works).
- **China/Avant-Garde exhibition, Beijing, February 1989**, at the **National Art Museum of China** — the prestigious museum that had refused the Stars in 1979. **Radical concepts and forms: installation, performance.**
- **Tiananmen Square Incident, Beijing, April–June 1989:** a massive student protest **triggered by the death of the reform-minded Party leader Hu Yaobang** → political stand-off → bloodshed → **migration and departure** of many, including artists.

## 4. Political Pop ★ — "to ridicule Mao's revolutionary legacy"

**Where "pop" comes from**
- **Pop art, USA, 1960s–70s. Andy Warhol (1928–1987), silkscreen prints.**
- Icons of **mass culture**, repeated: Campbell's Soup, Marilyn Monroe.
- **Warhol's *Mao* series, 1972–73** — made as the USA opened relations with China; Mao as a global icon.

**Formula: Political Pop = political icons + pop art.**

**The background it plays on**
- The **cult of Chairman Mao** in the Cultural Revolution (*Chairman Mao Goes to Anyuan*, 1968; Tang Xiaohe, 1972).
- The **cult of Mao in the Third World**: posters *Savior* (April 1968) and *Chairman Mao is the great liberator of the revolutionary people of the world* (April 1968), showing Africans with Mao's book.
- The **Red Guard as a political icon**.

### Zhang Hongtu (b. 1943; China-born, US-based) ★
- Born in **Gansu** to a **Muslim** family; graduated from the **Central Academy of Arts and Crafts, Beijing, 1969**.
- **Father labelled a Rightist / counter-revolutionary in 1957**; family carried the stigma of a traitor.
- At first fascinated by Maoist teaching, then **cynical**: saw book burnings and violence — **"not a revolution but a destruction of culture"**.
- **Moved to New York in 1982.** From the mid-1980s to the 1990s used **Mao's image** in paintings, sculptures and mixed-media installations.

| Work | What to remember |
|---|---|
| ***Chairman Mao* (1989, acrylic and collage)** | Rows of Mao's head, each altered: pigtails, a moustache, defaced, **tiger stripes**. ★★ **Ridicules Mao's political sanctity by turning him into a pop icon; revolutionary → unrevolutionary portraits; demystification of Mao's political power and legacy** |
| "Paper tiger" | **Something that looks terrifying but is in fact powerless. Used by Mao since 1946 for the USA and American imperialism** (*China Pictorial*, Aug 1950; poster *Expose the paper tiger*, 1958). Zhang turns Mao's own phrase back on him |
| ***The Last Banquet* (1989)** | Laser-printed **pages of the Red Book** and acrylic on canvas. After **Leonardo da Vinci's *Last Supper***, which Zhang first saw at 14. **Every figure at the table is Mao.** Jesus: "One of you will betray me." Mao: **"Who will betray me?"** Zhang: **"Mao always criticized other people and he never criticized himself."** |

- **Vivienne Tam** (New York-based Hong Kong designer; studied at PolyU): ***Mao* fashion collection, spring/summer 1995**, printing Zhang's images on clothing (stretch mesh). Mao moves from object of worship to **commercial icon** — another demystification.

### Wang Guangyi (b. 1955) ★
- Born in northeast China; trained as a painter; stayed in China.
- ***Great Criticism* series:** *Coca-Cola* (1994), *Chanel* (2001), *Rolex* (2005), also Ferrari.
- Method: **Cultural Revolution propaganda figures** (worker-peasant-soldier, Red Guards, PLA) **juxtaposed with international commercial logos**.
- He matches figure to brand: saluting Red Guard **girls** with Chanel (once "serve the people", now saluting a luxury brand); charging **soldiers** and diagonals for Ferrari (speed); soldier and old peasant gazing up at Rolex.
- ★ Slide: **China's economic opening since late 1978 → international commercial logos → appropriated → critical reflection on the relationship between politics and commerce.** In the Mao era politics came first; after opening, commerce, wealth and status took its place.
- His paintings now sell for over HKD 1 million at Christie's — part of the irony.

**Zhang vs Wang in one line:** Zhang is abroad and attacks **Mao's image** itself; Wang is in China and sets **revolutionary imagery against consumer brands**.

## 5. Global contemporary art space ★ — Chinese artists outside China

Artists who left around **1989** and became global figures.

**Challenges outside home** (three levels)

| Level | Challenge |
|---|---|
| **Basic: life** | Adapt to a new economic model, **capitalism**; overcome **language and cultural barriers** |
| **Artistic creation** | **Catch up** with Western contemporary art; **seek recognition** from Western society; **position oneself** in the globe |
| **Advanced: philosophical** | **Intervene in the Western-led global order** — "change the rules of the game" |

### Xu Bing (b. 1955) ★
- **Printmaking Department, Central Academy of Fine Arts, graduated 1981; master's 1987; moved to New York 1990; returned to Beijing 2008.** His education was delayed by the Cultural Revolution.

***Book from the Sky* (c. 1987–1991)**
- A huge **installation**: printed books on the floor, long scrolls hanging from the ceiling, panels on the walls.
- The characters **look Chinese but none can be read** — he invented them and hand-carved the blocks.
- Meaning: growing up among **big-character posters**, he came to **distrust text**: powerful for propaganda, yet policies and people's fates changed overnight. The work **demystifies the power of text**; standing inside it recalls being surrounded by dazibao.

***Square Word Calligraphy* (1993–1996)** ★
- **English words written with Chinese-style brushstrokes and arranged into square, character-like blocks.** Examples: "Square Word", "Art for the People", his signature "Xu Bing".
- ★ Slide: **"Xu reformed his mother tongue, the Chinese language, by reinventing it within the mechanics and logics of the English language."**
- Chinese readers who know no English cannot read it; **English readers can**, once they learn the system.
- Built on Chinese calligraphy teaching:
  - **The Eight Principles of Yong** (永): the eight most common strokes in standard script;
  - a self-made, **carved and printed textbook** (he is a printmaker): stroke order, how to hold the brush;
  - a red-outline **copybook** for tracing;
  - a **classroom installation** where visitors sit and practise; Xu was **invited by museums around the world** to teach it.
- ★ How it **intervenes in the Western-led order**: a marginalised immigrant with poor English becomes the **centre of museum attention**; Western visitors must learn **his** system, in an East Asian classroom posture. **The power dynamic is reversed.** It is **participatory** and joins traditional Chinese calligraphy with contemporary art practice.
- Hong Kong link: *Art in MTR* (2025), station names such as "Admiralty" and "Wan Chai" in Square Word.

### Cai Guoqiang (b. 1957) ★
- **The "explosion artist": gunpowder and pyrotechnics.**
- Born in **Quanzhou, Fujian**; graduated from the **Stage Art Department, Shanghai Theater Academy, 1985**.
- Invited by museums and exhibitions worldwide; the works are **ephemeral** events.

## 6. Conclusion slides ★
- **Globalization: a borderless world; a free flow of capital, human agents and ideas.**
- **Chinese artists adopted Western contemporary artistic techniques, mingling them with Chinese social, cultural and historical content and experience.**
